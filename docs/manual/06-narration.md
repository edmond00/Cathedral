# VI. Of Narration

Narration is what happens at a place. It is the principal mode of play and the one in which most of
the machinery of the preceding chapters is actually spent.

## 1. The scene and the point of view

A **location** is built as a set of **areas** — rooms, yards, tracks, clearings — joined by open
ways and by **connectors**, the latter being doors, stairs, cliffs, crossings, paths and the like.
An area holds **points of interest**, and a point of interest may hold **items**. Every area is of
one of four tenures — private, protected, public or wild — which has consequences of law (§7).

**Ways and paths.** Neighbouring areas of open ground are joined by a way that is simply walked: to
move into a neighbour is an act of difficulty 1. A connector that gates passage — a door, a climb, a
ford — has no such way beside it, and must itself be passed. A **path** is the shortcut of open
ground: a named way running past one or two areas to one lying two or three steps off, and following
it is a single act of difficulty 2, whatever its length, the one point more being the price of the
areas passed by. A path runs only through public and wild ground, never through a private area and
never across a gate, so that it is never a way round either. Not every location has one: each pair
of areas two steps apart is joined by a path with a chance of 30 in 100, each pair three steps apart
with 15 in 100, and no area has more than two.

**Known ways.** A way out already known — a neighbouring area already stood in, or a door, stair,
path or climb already gone by — is no longer an act. It is simply walked: a body goes, at no cost
and upon no roll, to any area already visited that known ways join to the one it stands in. Such a
walk never passes a door locked against the comer, and never steps from other ground into private,
so the private rooms it may return to are only those of the private ground the body already stands
in. It is not for that a walk through an empty place. Every area on the way is weighed as though the
body stood in it, the areas a path runs past among them, and in the first where an enemy or a
witness would confront it (§7) it stops and stands confronted; whatever it had set out to do there
is dropped. No roll is made at such a stop: flight alone is rolled.

A way *not* yet gone by remains an act, at its own difficulty. When such a way — a door, a stair, a
path, a climb or a crossing never taken — comes out upon ground already stood on, what the act
teaches is how the place joins together, in the stead of its ordinary lesson: taken with an enemy in
sight, by night, or on private ground, each teaches its own; otherwise it teaches the retracing of
known ways, or to a beast the following of its own trail.

What is known of a place — the areas stood in, the ways gone by, the things and persons an
observation took in — is the place's **mental map**, and lasts only as long as the map is remembered
(see [IX §1](09-routines.md)). A place forgotten is unknown again: none of its ways is known, and
nothing in it has a name.

A **point of view** is a pair: *where* the acting body stands, and *when* — the period of the day.
Both are scene state, and both decide what may be attempted. Moving between areas changes the first;
waiting changes the second.

A scene is **rebuilt from nothing on every arrival**, and is a pure function of the location's
identity: the same rooms, the same people and the same animals return identical, down to names and
belongings. What the visitor changed must therefore be recorded expressly, or it did not happen
(§9).

## 2. Acts, and what they come to

Nothing is ever done in the abstract. To seize, to climb, to strike is a *manner* of acting; an
**act** is always that manner brought to bear upon a particular thing, at a particular moment. The
same manner turned upon two different things is two different acts, and each is asked the same three
questions independently: whether it is possible here at all, how hard it would be, and whether it
would be a crime. All three answers depend on the circumstance and not on the manner alone, which is
why the same thing done in two rooms may be routine in one and a crime in the other.

What follows an act is a **consequence**: something in the world is different, and the change is
reported. The two go together — a consequence that reports nothing changed nothing that can be
observed. This is why the account rendered after an act is not decoration upon it but the whole
evidence of what it did.

**Play** is the one manner of acting that takes its whole character from its object. Stones skipped
across a creek, a rope swing ridden out over a bank, a hound scratched behind the ears and a heap of
hay jumped into share nothing but being done for their own sake, and so the thing played with
settles everything another manner settles for itself: what precisely is done, how hard it is, what
the body must be capable of (hands for a stone or a rope, Abstraction for a game with rules — see
[Chapter I §5](01-anatomy.md#5-the-three-capabilities)), what it teaches, and what a failure may
cost. A creature is played with only while it does not count the actor an enemy. **A successful
play changes nothing in the world**; its whole consequence is the modus mentis it teaches, which is
that play's own — the skipping of stones teaches the skipping of stones — and failing any the body
can hold, a general playfulness. About a quarter of the areas of the world hold something to be
played at.

## 3. The visit and its budget

Narration is not divided into turns. For the length of a visit the place lies open before the acting
body: what it has attended to stays attended to, what it has resolved to do waits to be done, and
nothing is set aside until the place is left. What limits it is its **noetic points**, being the
**encephalon's** measure. One point is spent by each of the following:

- an **observation**, the first of a visit like any other;
- a **deliberation**, with all its second thoughts (§5);
- a **reconsideration** (§5);
- an act **refused by a coded rule** (§6);
- an act found **implausible**;
- an implement **offered to an act and refused** (see [Chapter IV §7](04-items.md#7-implements));
- a thing **spoken of to a companion** (§10).

To attempt an act already resolved upon costs nothing, and neither does walking by known ways (§1).

**The pool is restored by success.** An act that succeeds returns the whole pool to the body that
performed it, there and then. It is restored likewise when a fight, a conversation, a bargain or a
spell of work has been had and the narration is taken up again, to every member of the party
standing where that took place. An act that **fails** restores nothing. A miss must cost something;
were the budget handed back, the very next attempt would be free.

Two things are deliberately outside the budget. **An implement accepted costs nothing** — it is
part of an act already deliberated upon, and to charge for it would put the acts that require an
implement beyond a small pool altogether. Only the refusal is charged, and the reckoning is
therefore not upon reaching for a thing but upon reaching for the wrong one. And **a successful
act's own resolution** costs nothing beyond the deliberation that produced it.

A body that has spent the pool may neither attend to nor deliberate upon anything further until an
act of its own succeeds. It may still attempt whatever it has already resolved upon, walk where it
knows the way, and leave. That is the intended shape, and it is why departure is gated on nothing
except somebody with a reason to stop it — an enemy, or a witness confronting the body (§7).

## 4. What a visit keeps

**Every thing in a place stands, to a given body, in one of three conditions.** It is **unknown**
when nothing of it is had but its kind — a way onward, a person, one who means harm, or a thing. It
is **known** when an earlier visit observed it and the place is still remembered
([IX §1](09-routines.md)): it then has its name, and may be attended to in particular. It is
**observed** when it has been attended to on this visit, by this body. Only what is observed may be
deliberated upon. What a body has observed stays observed for the rest of the visit, and is its own:
each member of a party keeps a separate account (§10). When the place is left the account is closed,
and what was observed in it remains known for as long as the place is remembered.

**The account is kept as it was made.** Each observation, each deliberation with its refusals and
second thoughts, each act and what it came to is set down under the thing it concerned, in the order
it happened, and may be read again at any time. An act resolved upon and not yet attempted **waits**:
it may be attempted at any later moment of the visit, so long as the world still allows it. When the
world no longer does — the thing taken, the person gone to bed, the way since walked — the act lapses
and is withdrawn.

**The account follows the world.** A person who moves takes with them all that was thought about
them, and is lost to sight while they stand where the body has never been. One who is killed is
struck from it, and the body they leave lies in it already observed, for every member standing where
it fell (§11). One who comes in at a noise, and one who confronts the body, are in it likewise
without having been looked at (§7).

**Nothing is offered twice while it waits.** What has been offered as an act is recorded by what the
actor perceives — *this manner of acting, upon this thing* — together with the particular form it
took, where the act admits of several (which trade was asked for, which third party one asked to be
presented to). An act is recorded when it is actually offered, and struck from the record once it
has been attempted or has lapsed, so that it may be resolved upon anew. A goal that deliberation
settled upon and the acting modus mentis then *refused* was never offered, and so remains available:
the refusal was that mind's, and asking again with another mind is precisely the move that ought to
work.

It remains available to every thinking modus mentis **but the one that chose it**. That mind is not
offered the same act again, for asked about the same object it would settle on the same goal and
meet the same refusal. A companion's modus mentis of the same name is a different mind, and is not
bound by it.

**An act once refused is not refused a second time.** Before it is offered again, it is tested
against the rules of choice that refuse outright: if no acting modus mentis the body holds could
carry it out under them — an examination where none of them observes — it is offered to no mind. If
some can, the next mind to choose it is offered only those as the means, and the one chosen answers
only with a degree of assent; it may not refuse.

**A mind that wanted nothing of an object is not asked twice.** A thinking modus mentis may judge
that nothing an object affords is worth doing, and say so. Its judgement does not vary — the same
mind asked about the same object returns the same answer — so to ask it again is to insist: the
second time, it may not decline, and settles on one of the goals offered. Another mind asked about
the same object may still decline it.

**The restoring of the pool wipes these slates.** Every refusal and every declining recorded since
the pool was last full is forgotten with it (§3), and each mind may be asked again as though for the
first time. The acts that wait are untouched.

## 5. The three steps of an act

**Observation.** To observe is to ask an observing modus mentis to attend to *one thing of a given
kind in a given area*. Which thing, among those of that kind there that the body has neither
observed nor remembers, is the mind's own choice; it renders what it finds, and that one thing is
thenceforth observed. A thing that is known is asked for in particular, and no other is chosen in
its place. Nothing is rendered of the area at large, and nothing is observed unasked: an arrival
attends to nothing. Observation requires presence — the body walks to the area first, by known
ways, and what it cannot so reach it cannot observe.

**The declining is spent once taken.** A mind asked to attend may answer instead that nothing of
that kind there draws it, or that the thing named does not. The answer costs its point as an
observation does, and stands in the account. The same mind asked again about the same kind in the
same area, or about the same thing, may not decline a second time, and must attend to something:
were each asking free to end in a declining, a thing in plain view could go unattended for a whole
visit. Another mind has a declining of its own. A will's turning from what does not begin its course
(§6) is a rule of its nature, not this declining, and is never spent. In recollection and in the
rising from exhaustion no mind declines at all.

**Contents.** A thing that holds two or more different things discloses them when it is observed:
each becomes known, though not observed, and is attended to in its own turn and at the cost of its
own point. Deliberation upon the holder is offered every act its contents afford beside its own;
upon one of its contents, only that one's.

**Deliberation.** A thinking modus mentis is asked what to want, among the acts the observed object
affords as the world now stands and that are not already waiting, nor already chosen by it and
refused, nor refused once and beyond every acting modus mentis held. Deliberation requires no
presence: a thing observed may be thought about from anywhere in the place, and is offered for
thought only while it affords some act. Its moral level and its speech narrow the list before
it is asked (§6).
Its answer is a goal, and the goal produces the act.

**Execution.** An act is attempted where its object is: the body walks there first, by known ways,
and an act whose walk is stopped is not attempted (§1). An acting modus mentis is asked whether it
is willing, and how willingly. Its degrees
of assent map to a modifier upon the difficulty, and its refusal cancels the act outright. Its moral
level may settle the answer before it is asked: a high one refuses a crime, a low one may not refuse
one (see §7); and one that speaks may not refuse a conversation (§6).

**Second thoughts.** A thinking whose proposal comes to nothing may be taken up again by the same
thinking modus mentis, as many times as its readiness to doubt allows
([Chapter II §1](02-memory.md#1-what-a-modus-mentis-is)): never at low, once at medium, twice at
high. A proposal comes to nothing when the
acting modus mentis refuses it, or when it is doubted, as below. Each refusal is rendered, and the
mind acknowledges it before deliberating anew. A refused goal is taken up again at once if another
acting modus mentis not yet tried for it — for a conversation, a speaking one, where any is held — is
one that no rule of choice forbids it to; the act is then carried out by one of those, which is not
asked whether it would rather not, as in §4, and the thinking ends upon an act. If none is, the goal
is struck off, and the mind deliberates upon another. When nothing remains the thinking ends, saying
so. Only a refusal given before an act is offered is deliberated anew: the coded rules (§6) judge the
act when it is attempted, and a witness in sight or an enemy in the room is not a reason to think
again but a reason the attempt fails. The whole chain is one thinking, and costs one noetic point.

**Doubt.** A thinking modus mentis of medium or high readiness may be doubted; one of low readiness
never is. While a further deliberation remains, the reasoning ends upon a question, and is answered — to hold to the proposal or not —
before the acting modus mentis is asked. Not to hold to it strikes off the goal
itself, with every acting modus mentis at once — the doubt is of the end, not of the means — and the
mind deliberates again upon another. Either answer is voiced by the thinking
modus mentis. Not to hold to it spends a further deliberation, as a refusal does; to hold to it
spends none. No doubt is offered on the last deliberation, which would otherwise end the thinking
upon nothing.

**Reconsideration.** An act already offered may be thought over again by a thinking modus mentis of
the player's choosing, at the cost of one noetic point. The mind so asked may hold to the act or
want no part of it, but proposes no other; a will holds to an act of its own course and to no
other. Holding to it, it gives the act to an acting modus mentis other than the one that offered
it, and only to one that no rule of choice forbids and no coded rule (§6) would refuse when the act
is attempted — so that an act which a witness in sight or an enemy in the room would forbid passes
to a discreet modus mentis, where one is held. That modus mentis does not decline it. The new act
takes the place of the old, which is kept in the account and may no longer be attempted. When the mind wants no part of it, or
no other acting modus mentis could carry it out, the act offered before stands as it was. It is not
offered in recollection nor in the rising from exhaustion, whose one act has one means.

**The die pool is the sum of the levels of every modus mentis in the chain** — the one that
observed, the one that deliberated, the one that acts — together with the whole level of any
implement combined, for which see [Chapter IV §7](04-items.md#7-implements). Never fewer than one
die.

The level counted is what the frame can presently bring to bear, not what was once learned: a wounded
source lowers the ceiling, and the ceiling binds the level
([Chapter II §3](02-memory.md#3-level-and-its-ceiling)). An injured body therefore casts fewer dice,
and one injured past a certain point casts none at all with that manner of the mind.

**The difficulty is the number of sixes required**, from nought to ten: the difficulty inherent in that
manner of acting upon that thing, adjusted by the acting modus mentis's degree of willingness — one
less when it is eager, one more when it is reluctant.

**An act of difficulty nought is not rolled.** Some manners of acting have nothing in them that can
go wrong, and are of difficulty nought by nature: to open a door that is not fastened, to go up or
down a stair, to sit and let the hour pass, and to go over to a person to speak — to present oneself,
to propose a trade, to ask for work, the use of a workshop or an introduction, or simply to talk; what
is at stake in those lies in the conversation that follows ([Chapter VII](07-social.md)). An act of
difficulty one carried out by an eager modus mentis falls to nought likewise. Such an act casts no
dice and succeeds, as a recollection does; it therefore never fails, and draws none of the
consequences of failure (§8). A reluctant modus mentis raises it to one, and it is rolled as any
other. **A crime is never of difficulty nought**: an act that is unlawful where and upon whom it is
done (§7) is of difficulty one at the least, whatever its manner and however eager its means, since
what a crime risks is the failure that is overheard, and an act that cannot fail could never be.

**Each die is cast independently; the act succeeds when the count of sixes reaches the difficulty.**
Nothing else enters the roll. Organs do not nudge it; they set the ceilings that let a modus mentis
be raised. Humors may bend the dice after they have fallen, as [Chapter III
§4](03-humors.md#4-the-transmuting-virtues) describes, and the count is taken again afterwards.

Whence a consequence worth stating plainly: **an act whose difficulty exceeds the pool that reached
it cannot succeed at all.** A difficulty of four demands four sixes, and three unraised modi mentis
cast three dice. Such acts require either an implement, or raised modi mentis, or a different chain.

**Every modus mentis in a chain that carried a successful act gains a point of experience**, and
most acts additionally teach a modus mentis of their own — the lesson of having done the thing.

**Sentiment.** An act that has resolved may be answered by a fourth modus mentis, which took no part
in performing it. Where the consequences of the act are of a kind that moves one of the dispositions
the body holds, that disposition renders humors of the passions into the spleen and gives its own
account of the matter — not of what was done, which the acting modus mentis has already related, but
of what it came to in the one who did it. The doctrine of it is in [Chapter III
§8](03-humors.md#8-the-humors-of-the-passions).

Two things follow that are worth stating here. The sentiment is settled **after** the consequences
and **before** anything the act may have set in motion, so a body that has struck the first blow of
a fight is moved by the blow before the fight begins. What an act sets in motion — a fight, a conversation, a spell at a bench, the losing of the way —
begins only when the account of the act has been taken in, the next thing being apt to bury it. And
the sentiment is **silent by default**: most acts come
to nothing any disposition answers, and a body that holds no disposition at all is never moved.

## 6. The two families of rule

Two distinct bodies of rule stand between a body and an act, and the distinction is not
presentational.

**Rules of action** judge an act already chosen. They **reject** it; the reason is given in the
acting modus mentis's own voice; and it **costs a point**. Circumstance belongs here — that man is
watching, that door needs a key, there is nowhere left to put a log. Nine such rules exist: a manner
of the mind the frame can no longer carry, a tongue too damaged to speak, an affinity too poor to
address, a crime refused before a witness, an act impossible under threat, an act impossible under a
confronting witness's eye, a missing required implement, a blow with nothing left to strike it, and a
carrying capacity already met.

The first stands ahead of the others because it alone concerns the frame rather than the
circumstance. One told that a witness is watching will try another way; one told that the arm is
ruined will not, and the refusal names the parts that failed and the wounds behind them.

**The missing implement alone leaves the act standing.** Its refusal says what was wanted, that bare
hands cannot do it, and which implement is wanting; the act may then be taken up again only with an
implement combined, never bare-handed a second time. Only when nothing combinable is carried is it
closed like any other refused act.

**A broken manner of the mind is offered and refused, not hidden.** It remains among those that may
be chosen to observe, to deliberate and to act, and the refusal is what informs of the injury —
withdrawing it silently would take a body's disciplines away with no account of where they had gone.
Speech alone is excepted: a reply is set down as a thing said, and there is no room in it for a
refusal, so a broken speaking modus mentis is passed over instead. When the last of them is passed
over, the rule of action that governs a damaged tongue answers for it — it asks two questions, whether
there is a voice and whether there is any manner of the mind left able to use it.

**Rules of choice** narrow what is *offered*. They **withhold**, silently, and **cost nothing**,
because nothing was refused. Character belongs here — that never occurred to me.

**The senses belong to the observing modi mentis.** In a body of the human anatomy, a thinking modus
mentis that does not bear the Observation function is never offered any of the four senses as a goal
— to examine, to contemplate, to listen or to smell. A beast is under no such rule: its every
thinking modus mentis is offered the senses, which are the greater part of what a beast may do. On
the acting side the rule is narrower and holds for every anatomy: an acting modus mentis without the
Observation function, chosen to carry out an examination, refuses at once and is not asked — the
refusal states that it would act rather than stand and observe. The other three senses are carried
out by whichever acting modus mentis is chosen.

**Principle refuses a crime before it is offered.** A high-moral acting modus mentis chosen to carry
out a crime refuses at once and is not asked, while the act is still being deliberated — no act is
offered, and no point is spent on an attempt. The refusal is remembered as any other (see §4): the
same thinking modus mentis is not offered that goal again, and another that chooses it is
offered as means only those that would not refuse it.

**Speech turns toward people.** A modus mentis bearing the Speaking function puts conversation
first, at every step of the chain it takes part in. Observing, it does not decline when what it is asked to attend to is a person. Thinking,
when the observed object affords any conversation, only the conversations are offered — save that a
low-moral mind already narrowed to crimes keeps the crimes, its morality being consulted first.
When the goal is a conversation, the acting modi mentis that speak are the only means offered, if any
is held; and such a one cannot refuse a conversation, though it may undertake it reluctantly. A rule
of action that forbids the conversation itself — a listener who is hostile, a tongue with nothing
left to say — still applies. Where nobody can be spoken to, none of this narrows anything.

**A will goes only its own way.** A will (see [Chapter II §7](02-memory.md#7-what-the-modi-mentis-are-for))
is reckoned the shortest course of acts from where the body stands to an act of the kind it wants,
and at every step of the chain it attends only to the first act of such a course — of several
equally short, any. In what it says, a person on its course is named as the narration names them
elsewhere. Asked to attend to a thing in particular, it does so if the thing affords that first act, and
otherwise declines, naming the act that would begin the course; asked to attend to one of a kind, it
attends to the first of that kind which affords it, and to none where none does.
Deliberating, it is less strict than in attending. Of the acts the object affords, those that begin a
course to what it wants of at most three acts, the wanted act included — or of one no longer than the
shortest course the place offers, however long that is — are its candidates; it is offered only the
candidate whose course is shortest, and where several courses are equally short it chooses among
their first acts itself. An act already waiting is no candidate
([§4](#4-what-a-visit-keeps)), so a second deliberation upon the same object takes the next course through
it, within the same bounds, and with none left the will proposes nothing and lets the object go. On an
object that affords no candidate it proposes nothing, saying only
what it wants; it admits no doubt, its goal being set by its course, and
tries again — once, a will being of medium readiness — only with another means for an act on its way. In either office it gives its course as its reason. Where the course does not exist
within the place, it declines in both and says that only leaving offers what it wants. Bearing the
Action function, it is its own means for the act it wants, and is not asked how willingly: it is
eager for it, and the difficulty falls by one; an act on its way is carried out by
whichever means is chosen for it, as any goal is. A will cannot refuse an act that would be its candidate — though a rule
of action, which judges circumstance and not character, still may.

**A visit made for one want.** From the world a will may be sent to seek one of its wants among the
places within range: they are examined in order of the cost of the road to each, the nearest first,
and the first in which the course to that want exists from the place's way in, at the hour of
arrival, is proposed as the journey's end. A visit reached by that journey is made for that want, and
while it lasts the will reckons its course to that want alone, however long — the course it
attends by, and the things it will attend to. Deliberating, it takes
first a course through the object to that want, whatever its length; only where the object affords
none are its other wants candidates, and then only by a course of at most three acts. Where no
course to the sought want remains in the place — the thing done, or gone — the will reckons by all
its wants again. Any other visit, a later one to the same place among them, is reckoned as above.

The distinction has a practical edge: an act blocked by a rule of action is an act you attempted and
were stopped from completing, and you pay for the attempt.

## 7. Of crime and of being seen

**Legality is contextual.** No manner of acting is a crime in itself. Standing in another's private
space makes *anything* done there a trespass, and that test applies to everything at once. It is
never hidden: throughout a visit the narration declares whether the ground underfoot is private, and
private ground teaches no routine (see [IX](09-routines.md)). Beyond
it, each act that can be a crime has its own condition — striking someone who already counts you an
enemy is not a crime, for the quarrel was declared before the blow; forcing the lock of a public
storehouse is nobody's privacy.

**Ground has four tenures.** *Private* ground is somebody's, and makes every act a trespass. It is
never entered from other ground unawares: the only ways in are a door locked against the comer —
the door of a house, or of any room that is somebody's, the rooms off a great building's passages
among them — and ways that are themselves an intrusion, a window slipped through or a wall scaled. No open way,
stair or unlocked door leads from other ground into private, though within private ground the ways
between rooms are open. What lies there is never free to take: to carry it off is theft, whoever has
let the body in.
*Protected* ground is a keeper's floor open to callers: every room not private within a building whose
entry door stands open by day and is shut at night — the hall of a workshop, a shop or an inn, the
passages and public rooms of a temple or a castle. To stand there is no trespass, and an act that
leaves the place as it was — speech, the senses, waiting, walking — is no crime. An act that lays hands
on the place is a crime until a living master of the building has given leave: taking what lies there,
working its stations, cutting, extracting and breaking. With the leave these are lawful, and the leave
once given is kept; but it is asked only by a body holding a trade whose station stands in that building
([X §4](10-crafting.md)), so that elsewhere what lies on such a floor is to be bought or stolen. A floor
whose master is dead or mute asks no leave of anybody. A roof is outside the door, and is public.
*Public* ground is everybody's: the streets, yards and fields of a farm, a village or a city. *Wild*
ground is nobody's: the open ground of a forest, a plain, a cave, a coast, a mountain, a peak and
the climate biomes. A store kept there — a woodshed, a net shed, an ore store — is somebody's stock
and remains public. The tenure decides one thing besides trespass: whether a beast belongs to
anybody. Striking or killing a beast that is not already an enemy is a crime on any ground but the
wild, the creature being taken to be somebody's; on wild ground it is hunting, and no
crime at all. A creature small enough to be taken in the hand is caught or crushed rather than
struck, and neither is a crime against it on any ground; private ground remains a trespass. A
person is never game: every crime against a person is a crime on wild ground as anywhere.

**Morality has four distinct behaviours**, two on each side of the chain:

| | A thinking modus mentis | An acting modus mentis |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Crimes are dropped from its list of goals. Filtering everything away is a legitimate result. | Refuses at once, unasked, before the act is offered. |
| **Low** | If any goal on offer is a crime, **only** the crimes are offered, and it may not decline them all: it chooses one. | Cannot refuse: the option to decline is withheld. |

Both halves are needed, because the goal and the means are two separate decisions: a scrupulous
skill may still be chosen to carry out a goal some other mind settled on.

**Being caught is two separate questions**, and keeping them apart is what makes discretion mean
anything.

*May I act at all?* Asked of **raw** proximity by the rules of action. Somebody in the room blocks a
non-discreet modus mentis outright. A **discreet** modus mentis may always attempt — that permission
is the whole of what discretion buys at close quarters. The acts that answer a confronting witness
(below) are exempt: done to the one already watching, they leave nobody to be caught by. Nor may a
discreet modus mentis decline an act that only its discretion permits here — a crime in sight of a
witness, any act but facing the threat while an enemy stands in the room, any act but answering a
confronting witness. It may undertake such an act reluctantly; it may not refuse it, save where
principle refuses it first. Out of everybody's sight it declines as any other mind.

*What does failing cost?* Asked of **effective** proximity, where discretion applies. Discretion
silences a body **through a wall**, never **in the same room**: it downgrades a Visual presence not
at all, and an Audio presence to nothing.

Three rungs, identical for a witness and for an enemy save in what the top rung is:

| Effective proximity | A witness | An enemy |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual** | Caught in the act: a confrontation, which is a conversation, and whose failure is a fight. | They attack, on their own initiative. |
| **Audio** | **They come to look.** They move into the area. | The same. |
| **None** | Nothing. | Nothing. |

The middle rung is an approach, not a verdict. Nothing is yet said; what it costs is that somebody
now stands in the room, having come on the body's account, and that is a confrontation (below).
Earshot is not the walkable graph but the whole **section** an area belongs to — a building, or the
stretch of open ground it lies in, for a door is a gate and not a wall. Being heard is announced as
a consequence of the failed act, and the one who came stands thereafter in the account of every
member in that area, observed without having been looked at: an enemy as the one who came looking,
a witness as the one who came to see what the noise was.

**Under threat, and confronted.** Two presences in the area stop a body short, and they are kept
deliberately alike. An **enemy** in the area permits only the acts that answer them — to strike, to
kill, to appease, to reconcile, to tame — and any act that fails before them begins a fight, the
enemy striking first. A **confronting witness** is one standing in the area with a reason to stop the
body: either they came on its account, drawn by a heard crime, or the ground is private and they see
the trespass every act there is. A person merely standing in a public place confronts nobody. A
confronting witness permits only striking at them, killing them, or going up to them to explain
oneself, and any act that fails before them, lawful or not, is caught red-handed: the confrontation,
worded after the crime they heard, or trespass where they heard none. To explain oneself opens that
same confrontation by one's own choice, and every check within it is then one six easier than when
one is collared, though never easier than a single six: one who comes forward is half believed.
Fumbling the approach is itself a slip before the witness, and one is collared after all. In both cases a **discreet** modus mentis may still attempt any act,
for the permission to act at close quarters is what discretion buys; it does not buy safety, and a
discreet act that fails in the same room is seen.

**What a confrontation closes.** While either presence stands over a body, that body may attend to
nobody and nothing but the one confronting it — who is in its account already, without having been
looked at — may deliberate upon them alone, may not walk away, and speaks to no companion. The acts
it had resolved upon before, in the area where it stands, still wait and may still be attempted, to
be judged as above. Somebody asleep confronts nobody and witnesses nothing: a sleeper is woken, or
stepped past. The other members of a party, standing elsewhere, are not confronted by it.

**Departure.** A place is left as it was entered, by its one way in, which lies beyond the area the
place was entered by. To leave is to walk there: from the area stood in, back along the ways already
known, to that area and out. Every member of a party leaves by it, each from where it stands, those
standing together going together; and the departure is a walk like any other, so that a member who
would be confronted on the way is stopped there (§1), and the party is then divided between those
already out and those still within.

**Flight.** While either presence stands in the area the free departure becomes a **runaway**: one
roll of as many dice as the feet's runaway score, needing a single six, which the humors may bend
as they bend any other roll. Failure begins the fight with an enemy, or the confrontation with a
witness. Success sets the body walking for the way in, and the walk is checked the rest of the way
out: every further area it passes through — the areas a path runs past among them — is weighed as
though the body stood in it, and wherever an enemy or a witness would confront it there, it is
stopped and rolls again on the same terms. Each success carries it on to the next such area, or out
of the place when none is left. A failure at such a stop forces nothing: the body stays in the area
where it was stopped, the noetic points are not restored, and whoever stopped it stands in its
account — and in the area with it, so that leaving is a runaway once more.

A confrontation ends with the conversation it opens: a witness who came on the body's account then
returns to the day they were drawn from, unless the conversation has made an enemy of them.

**Found waiting.** The householder who comes in at nightfall to find the body waiting in their house
(see [Chapter V §8](05-travel.md#8-the-periods-of-the-day)) confronts it as any witness does, and
the same three ways open the conversation — a failed act before them, a failed runaway, or going up
to explain oneself. The conversation is not the one a crime opens. It asks why the body is still
there and whether it is to be kept the night, and every resolution in it that rolls stands on the gravest of the
ladders ([Chapter VII §2](07-social.md#2-the-conversation)). To win it is to be allowed to stay:
for the rest of the visit, standing anywhere in that building is no trespass, and nobody in it
confronts the body for being there. To lose it is to be shown the door, which ends the visit. The
leave covers presence and nothing else. The rooms remain somebody's, so to take from them is still
theft and to force a locked door in them is still a crime, though an unlocked one may be opened; no
routine is learned there; and another building is another house. An act that is a crime of its own,
failing before the householder, opens the confrontation that crime opens, and not this one.

**Enmity persists across visits.** Walking away from a fight ends the fight, not the quarrel.

What a crime costs is what it costs at the time, and what the person who saw it thinks of you
afterwards. Nothing is written down, and no authority pursues you beyond the room.

## 8. Failure and its wages

A failed act costs, in order:

1. **The budget**, which a failure does not restore (§3).
2. **A social consequence**, read off effective proximity as §7 describes: at most one of caught,
   attacked, or approached. These are three rungs of one ladder, not a set of independent flags.
3. **A penalty**, drawn at hazard from what that manner of acting risks. No failure goes without one.

A penalty is of four kinds:

- **A wound**, where the attempt is one that can hurt its maker. It is stamped with the day and can
  therefore heal.
- **The way lost**, for the manners of acting that find a way — walking, following a path or a
  quarry, setting out for a distant prospect. The visit ends with the act, and the party stands again
  in the open country, outside the place.
- **The hour wasted**, for the hard and the slow — a climb, a swim, a crossing, a labour. The period
  of the day advances by one, and all that follows the hour follows it.
- **The thread lost**: one noetic point beyond the point the act already spent. Since the budget is
  not restored by a failure, the point stays lost until an act succeeds. It is what the
  manners of acting that hazard nothing else — the senses, speech, waiting — cost when they fail.

**A penalty that cannot take hold is drawn again** from the rest of the manner's risks. The way
cannot be lost where somebody saw or heard the failure, for a confrontation outranks it: the one who
caught you does not let you wander off, and the one who heard you comes to a room you are still in.
The hour cannot be wasted where the failure was seen, and so begins a fight or a confrontation, for
that quarrel is had there and then and not an hour later; a failure merely heard may still waste it.
The thread cannot be lost with no point left to lose. A wound must be one the body can bear; a
generic graze is borne as the acting body's own graze of the same kind, and a named injury the body
has no part for is drawn again.

A blow aimed at somebody is the exception to the whole of the above, and the exception is worth
stating: **a swing that misses begins the fight regardless**, and the ladder does not run, a quarrel
one has started outranking any consequence one's clumsiness invited. What the miss costs is the
initiative. See [Chapter VIII §5](08-fighting.md#5-the-opening-blow).

## 9. What a place remembers

Because a scene is rebuilt identically on every arrival, only what is expressly recorded survives.
Four facts are:

- **Affinity** — what each person here thinks of you.
- **Enmity** — who here counts you an enemy, which is *separate* from affinity and moves
  independently of it.
- **Depletion** — which gatherable places have been stripped, and when.
- **The departed** — everyone this place has lost for good. Departure is permanent: a replacement
  drawn from the same seed would be the very individual who left, so the place stays emptier.

Corpses, wounds dealt, and forced doors are deliberately **not** recorded. They are one visit's
business.

## 10. The party in a place

**Each member of a party is its own body in the place.** All enter by the same area; thereafter each
stands where it was last taken, and a move moves only the one who made it. Each observes,
deliberates and acts for itself — spending **its own** noetic points, drawing upon **its own** modi
mentis, and keeping **its own** account of what it has observed (§4). Which member is attended to
may be changed at any moment and at no cost. Time alone is common: the hour is one hour for all.

A companion may be a beast, and this is where the capabilities of [Chapter I
§5](01-anatomy.md#5-the-three-capabilities) bite hardest. Every gate consults **the acting body**,
not the protagonist. A beast is offered no conversation, no lock, no implement and no letters —
about half of everything there is to do — while tracking, scenting, going places and fighting remain
open to it.

**A conversation with the people of a place is the protagonist's.** No companion, of whatever kind,
opens one: its replies, the regard it earns and whatever it settles are the protagonist's. Two acts
besides consult the protagonist regardless of who performs them, being the two that concern the
party itself: the taming of a beast and the proposal that someone join.

**Speech between members.** A member holding a speaking modus mentis may speak of a thing it has
observed to a companion standing in the same area or in one adjoining it. It costs the speaker a
point. What was said is set down under the thing in the speaker's account, and the thing enters the
listener's account as observed, with those words in the place of an observation of its own: the
listener deliberates upon it from what it was told. Speaking does not change which member is
attended to.

**A fight takes those who stand where it breaks out**, and nobody else; whoever is fought counts
those members enemies thereafter. The protagonist may be absent from it. Lost with the protagonist
in it, it ends everything ([Chapter VIII](08-fighting.md)); lost by companions fighting alone, they
are dead, and the protagonist goes on.

**The party is kept current.** One who joins in the course of a visit stands where the member who
won them stands, having observed nothing. One who dies leaves their body where they stood (§11).

## 11. What a killing leaves

A death leaves a **corpse** in the area it happened in — an ordinary point of interest, not a place
to be entered. Its contents are the harvestable parts, and they are folded into the corpse's own
affordances, so one attention upon the body offers everything that may be cut from it. Identical
parts collapse to a single goal, each cutting removing one.

**A body yields between four and eight parts.** Fewer than four and a carcass does not repay the
approach it costs — a killing, a blade, and a point of the budget for every attempt; more than eight
and the parts run past what one attention can offer and past what may be borne away. Duplicates are
counted in the eight but not in the goals, so a body carrying three cuts of meat offers one act of
cutting that may be taken three times. What differs between one creature and another is which parts
and how many of each, never what those parts are called: the same flesh and the same bone are had
from every body, and a bear is a bear by giving eight where a smaller beast gives four.

A creature small enough to be taken in the hand rather than slain yields two parts or three, granted
whole at the taking rather than cut out one at a time. Crushing such a creature leaves nothing.

**A human is opened like any other body**, and no exception is made for the kind — the same flesh, with
the hair from the head and the skull from beneath it. A human additionally leaves a separate holding
of what they carried. The division needs no reasoning
about particular items: everything in a corpse is flesh, and the acts of cutting take it while the
acts of seizing refuse it; everything in the belongings beside it is cloth and steel, and the
reverse holds.

**Cutting requires a blade combined with the act** ([Chapter IV §7](04-items.md#7-implements)), as
does the killing that yields a body without a fight. A carcass is opened with an edge or not at all,
and a body lacking the hands to hold one is barred from both acts entirely rather than being offered
them and refused.

**A fallen companion leaves the same**, by species and by what they carried, with no distinction
made for having been of the party. What is cut from a companion who was a beast is what would be cut
from any beast of that kind.

**A body is observed where it falls.** It needs no looking for: it enters the account of every
member standing in the area, already observed, each body in the order it fell, and may be
deliberated upon at once. No mind observed it, and none is therefore counted in a roll made upon it
beyond those that deliberate and act.
