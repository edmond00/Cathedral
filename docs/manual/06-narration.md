# VI. Narration

Narration is what happens inside a place: looking around, deciding what to do, and doing it. It is
the main mode of play, and it is where most of the rules of the earlier chapters are applied.

**In short**

1. A character in a place has a budget of **noetic points** (attention points) = ⌊encephalon score ÷ 2⌋ + 3; every observation, deliberation and attempted act costs 1 point, and a successful act refills the budget (§3).
2. Every act passes through a chain of modi mentis: one observes, one thinks, one acts (§5).
3. The die pool is the sum of the levels of the modi mentis in the chain, plus any implement; the act succeeds when the number of sixes reaches the difficulty (§5).
4. An act of difficulty 0 is not rolled and always succeeds; a crime is never below difficulty 1 (§5).
5. A failed act refills nothing, may bring a witness or an enemy down on the character, and always carries a penalty: a wound, the way lost, the hour wasted, or a lost noetic point (§7, §8).
6. A place is rebuilt identically on every visit; only affinity, enmity, depletion and the dead are remembered (§9).

## 1. Places and the point of view

A place is laid out as areas joined by ways, and the character always stands in one area at one
time of day.

**Rules**

1. A **location** (a place on the world map) is built as a set of **areas**: rooms, yards, tracks, clearings.
2. Areas are joined by open ways and by **connectors**: doors, stairs, cliffs, crossings, paths and the like.
3. An area holds **points of interest** (things that can be looked at and acted on), and a point of interest may hold **items**.
4. Every area has one of four **tenures** (who the ground belongs to): private, protected, public or wild. Tenure decides what is a crime (§7).
5. A scene is rebuilt from nothing on every arrival, and depends only on which location it is: the same rooms, people and animals return identical, down to names and belongings.
6. So whatever a visit changed must be stored explicitly, or it did not happen (§9).

### Ways and paths

1. Neighbouring areas of open ground are joined by a way that is simply walked.
2. Moving into a neighbouring area is an act of difficulty 1.
3. A connector that gates passage (a door, a climb, a ford) has no open way beside it, and must itself be passed.
4. A **path** is a named shortcut across open ground: it runs past 1 or 2 areas to an area 2 or 3 steps away.
5. Following a path is a single act of difficulty 2, whatever its length; the extra point is the price of the areas passed by.
6. A path runs only through public and wild ground. It never runs through a private area and never across a gate, so it is never a way around either.
7. Each pair of areas 2 steps apart is joined by a path with a chance of 30 in 100; each pair 3 steps apart, with a chance of 15 in 100.
8. No area has more than 2 paths, and some locations have none.
9. The areas a path runs past are seen in passing, not stood in. Following a path makes the path a known way (below); it does not make the open ways it ran beside known.

### Known ways

1. A way is **known** once it has been used: the open way to a neighbouring area, or a door, stair, path, climb or crossing.
2. Standing in 2 neighbouring areas does not make the way between them known. Each may have been reached by another way.
3. Walking by known ways is not an act. It costs no noetic point and is not rolled.
4. A character can walk to any area that known ways join to the area it stands in.
5. Such a walk never passes a door locked against the character, and never steps from other ground into private ground. The only private rooms it can return to are those of the private ground the character already stands in.
6. Every area on the way is judged as if the character stood in it, including the areas a path runs past.
7. In the first area where an enemy or a witness would confront the character (§7), the walk stops. The character stands there confronted, and what it set out to do is dropped.
8. No roll is made at such a stop. Only running away is rolled (§7).
9. A way not yet used is still an act, at its own difficulty.
10. When a way never used leads to an area already stood in, the act teaches how the place joins up instead of its usual modus mentis. With an enemy in sight, by night, and on private ground each teach their own. Otherwise it teaches the retracing of known ways, or, to a beast, the following of its own trail.

### The mental map

1. What is known of a place (the areas stood in, the ways used, the things and people observed) is the place's **mental map**.
2. It lasts only as long as the map is remembered (see [Chapter IX §1](09-routines.md)).
3. A forgotten place is unknown again: none of its ways are known, and nothing in it has a name.
4. The areas stood in and the ways used are shared by the whole party. A member can walk by a way another member used.
5. The things and people observed are not shared. Each is known only to the member who observed it, or who was told of it (§10).

### The point of view

1. A **point of view** is a pair: *where* the acting body stands, and *when*, meaning the period of the day.
2. Both are part of the scene, and both decide what may be attempted.
3. Moving between areas changes the first; waiting changes the second.

## 2. Acts and consequences

An **act** is one manner of acting (seizing, climbing, striking) aimed at one particular thing, at
one moment.

**Rules**

1. The same manner of acting aimed at two different things is two different acts.
2. Each act is judged separately on three questions: whether it is possible here at all, how hard it is, and whether it is a crime.
3. All three answers depend on the circumstance, not on the manner of acting alone. The same act can be routine in one room and a crime in the next.
4. What follows an act is a **consequence**: something in the world changes, and the change is reported.
5. A consequence that reports nothing changed nothing that can be observed. The account given after an act is the whole evidence of what it did.

### Play

**Play** is the one manner of acting whose details come entirely from the thing played with.

1. Examples of play: skipping stones across a creek, riding a rope swing over a bank, scratching a hound behind the ears, jumping into a heap of hay.
2. The thing played with decides what exactly is done, how hard it is, what the body must be capable of, what it teaches, and what a failure costs.
3. For example, a stone or a rope needs hands, and a game with rules needs Abstraction (see [Chapter I §5](01-anatomy.md#5-capabilities)).
4. A creature can be played with only while it does not count the actor an enemy.
5. A successful play changes nothing in the world. Its only consequence is the modus mentis it teaches.
6. Each play teaches its own modus mentis: skipping stones teaches the skipping of stones. If the body cannot hold that one, it teaches a general playfulness instead.
7. About a quarter of the areas in the world hold something to play with.

## 3. The visit and noetic points

Narration is not divided into turns. For the whole visit, what a character has observed stays
observed and what it has decided to do waits to be done. The limit is its budget of noetic points.

**Rules**

1. The budget is the character's **noetic points** = ⌊encephalon score ÷ 2⌋ + 3. The encephalon is the brain region; its score is a region score ([Chapter I §6](01-anatomy.md#6-derived-values)).
2. Each member of a party has its own budget (§10).
3. A successful act refills the whole budget of the character that performed it, at once.
4. The budget is also refilled after a fight, a conversation, a trade or a spell of work, for every party member standing in the area where it took place.
5. A failed act refills nothing.
6. Reason for rule 5: a miss must cost something. If the budget were handed back, the next attempt would be free.
7. Accepting an implement for an act costs nothing. It is part of an act already deliberated on, and charging for it would put acts that need an implement out of reach of a small budget.
8. Attempting an act costs 1 noetic point, whatever comes of it. A successful act then refills the budget (rule 3), so only an attempt that does not succeed keeps its point spent.
9. Reaching one act from nothing therefore takes 3 points: 1 to observe, 1 to deliberate, 1 to attempt. The smallest budget is 3, so every character can reach at least one act. Walking by known ways (§1) costs nothing.
10. A character with no noetic point left can observe nothing, deliberate on nothing and attempt nothing, including the acts it had already decided on.
11. It can only leave the place, or run away when it is confronted (§7).
12. In a party, a member with no noetic point left cannot be played until its budget refills. When the member being played spends its last point, play passes to a member who still has one, a member standing in the same area first (§10).
13. Leaving is blocked only by someone with a reason to stop the character: an enemy, or a confronting witness (§7).
14. Nothing costs a noetic point in the two opening scenes of a run: the childhood recollection, and getting up after it.

**Each of these costs 1 noetic point:**

1. An observation, including the first of a visit (§5).
2. A deliberation, with all its second thoughts (§5).
3. A reconsideration (§5).
4. An attempt at an act, whether it is refused by a rule of action (§6), judged implausible, failed or successful.
5. An implement offered for an act and refused (see [Chapter IV §7](04-items.md#7-implements)). Only the refusal is charged: the cost is for reaching for the wrong thing, not for reaching.
6. Speaking of a thing to a companion (§10).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Noetic points | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 5 | 14 |

## 4. What a visit keeps

Everything a character observes, decides and does in a place is kept for the whole visit, in a
**record** of its own.

### The three states of a thing

1. To a given character, every thing in a place is unknown, known or observed.
2. **Unknown**: only its kind is known (a person, an enemy or a thing) and the area it stands in, or the container that holds it (§5). A view of distant ground is a thing of the area it is seen from.
3. Kind and area are known for every area of the place, including areas never entered.
4. **Known**: this character observed it on an earlier visit, or was told of it by a companion (§10), and the place is still remembered ([Chapter IX §1](09-routines.md)). It has its name, and can be observed by name. A thing known to one party member is not known to another.
5. **Observed**: this character has observed it during this visit.
6. Only an observed thing can be deliberated on.
7. A thing stays observed for the rest of the visit.
8. Each party member has its own record (§10): what one member observed is not observed by another.
9. When the place is left, the record is closed. What a character observed stays known to that character for as long as the place is remembered.

### The record

1. Each observation, each deliberation with its refusals and second thoughts, and each act with its outcome is recorded under the thing it concerned, in the order it happened. It can be read again at any time.
2. An act decided on and not yet attempted **waits**. It can be attempted at any later moment of the visit, as long as the world still allows it.
3. When the world no longer allows it (the thing was taken, the person went to bed, the way has been walked), the act lapses and is withdrawn.
4. A person who moves keeps everything recorded about them, wherever they go in the place.
5. A person who is killed is removed from the record. Their corpse enters it already observed, for every party member standing in the area (§11).
6. Someone who comes because of a noise also enters the record already observed (§7). Anyone else who confronts the character does not: they must be observed like anything else.

### What has been offered as an act

1. An offered act is recorded as the actor sees it: this manner of acting, on this thing, together with the particular form it took where there are several (which trade was asked for, which third person one asked to be introduced to).
2. An act is recorded only when it is actually offered, and no act is offered twice while it waits.
3. Once the act has been attempted or has lapsed, it is removed, and can be decided on again.
4. A goal chosen in deliberation that the acting modus mentis then refused was never offered, so it stays available. Asking again with a different modus mentis is exactly the move that ought to work.
5. It stays available to every thinking modus mentis except the one that chose it. That one would choose the same goal again and meet the same refusal, so it is not offered that act again.
6. A companion's modus mentis of the same name is a different mind and is not bound by rule 5.

### An act refused once

1. Before an act refused once is offered again, it is tested against the rules of choice that refuse outright (§6).
2. If no acting modus mentis the body holds could carry it out under those rules (for example an examination when none of them has the Observation function), it is offered to no modus mentis.
3. If some can, the next modus mentis that chooses it is offered only those as the means.
4. The acting modus mentis chosen then gives only a degree of assent (§5); it may not refuse.

### A mind that wanted nothing

1. A thinking modus mentis may judge that nothing a thing offers is worth doing, and say so.
2. Its judgement does not vary: asked about the same thing, it would give the same answer. So it is not asked about that thing again while the judgement stands.
3. A thing can be declined this way only once. The next thinking modus mentis asked about it may not decline, and must choose one of the goals offered.
4. Three other answers are treated the same way, and the modus mentis that gave one is not asked the same again: a will (§6) that finds no course through the thing; a modus mentis with nothing left to try on it; and a modus mentis that, reconsidering an act (§5), rejected it or found no other modus mentis to carry it out.
5. A will's answer depends on the place as it stood. It is forgotten as soon as anything in the place changes.
6. The other answers last until the budget refills.

### When the budget refills

1. Every refusal and every declining recorded since the budget was last full is forgotten when the budget refills (§3).
2. Every modus mentis can then be asked again as if for the first time.
3. Waiting acts are not affected.

## 5. Observing, thinking and acting

Every act is produced by a chain of modi mentis (learned habits of mind; see
[Chapter II §1](02-memory.md#1-what-a-modus-mentis-is)): one with the Observation function picks
something to look at, one with the Thinking function picks a goal, and one with the Action function
carries it out.

### Observation

1. To observe is to ask an observing modus mentis to observe 1 thing of a given kind in a given area.
2. The modus mentis chooses which thing, among the things of that kind in that area that the character has neither observed nor knows (§4).
3. It describes what it finds, and that thing is then observed.
4. A known thing is asked for by name, and no other is chosen in its place.
5. A way is asked for by the 2 areas it joins: the open way between them, a door, a stair, a path, a climb or a crossing. The modus mentis does not choose among the ways out of an area.
6. When 2 or more ways that the character neither knows nor has observed join the same 2 areas, the modus mentis chooses which of them to observe. A way that is known, or the only one between its 2 areas, is asked for by itself.
7. A way is the same way from both its ends. It is observed once, from the end the character stands at.
8. Nothing is described of the area as a whole, and nothing is observed unasked. Arriving in a place observes nothing.
9. Observing needs presence. The character first walks to the area by known ways (§1), and cannot observe what it cannot reach that way.

### Declining to observe

1. A modus mentis asked to observe may answer that nothing of that kind there draws it, that nothing a container holds does, or that the named thing does not. This is **declining**.
2. Declining costs 1 noetic point, like an observation, and is recorded (§4).
3. The modus mentis that declined is not asked the same again.
4. A question can be declined only once. The next modus mentis asked about the same kind in the same area, the same container's contents, the ways between the same 2 areas, or the same thing, may not decline and must observe something.
5. Reason for rule 4: if each could decline in turn, a thing in plain view could go unobserved for a whole visit.
6. A character with only 1 observing modus mentis cannot ask again about what that one declined until the budget refills (§4).
7. A will turning away from what does not begin its course (§6) is part of what a will is, not this declining, and does not use it up. The will is not asked the same again until the place changes, and a modus mentis asked after it may still decline.
8. No modus mentis declines in the two opening scenes of a run: the childhood recollection, and getting up after it.

### Contents

1. A thing that holds 2 or more different things reveals how many when it is observed, and nothing else about them: each is unknown (§4).
2. To observe one is to ask an observing modus mentis to observe 1 thing the container holds. The modus mentis chooses which, among those the character has not observed, and each observation costs its own noetic point.
3. Deliberation on the container is offered every act its contents allow, as well as its own.
4. Deliberation on one of its contents is offered only that content's acts.

### Deliberation

1. A thinking modus mentis is asked what to want, among the acts the observed thing allows as the world now stands.
2. Left out of that list: acts already waiting; acts this modus mentis already chose and saw refused; acts refused once that no acting modus mentis held can carry out (§4).
3. Deliberation needs no presence: an observed thing can be deliberated on from anywhere in the place.
4. A thing is offered for deliberation only while it allows some act.
5. Its moral level and whether it speaks narrow the list before it is asked (§6).
6. Its answer is a goal, and the goal produces the act.

### Execution

1. An act is attempted where its object is. The character first walks there by known ways, and if the walk is stopped (§1) the act is not attempted.
2. An acting modus mentis is asked whether it is willing, and how willingly.
3. Its answer is one of three **degrees of assent**, or a refusal. The table gives the effect.
4. A refusal cancels the act outright.
5. Its moral level may settle the answer before it is asked: a high one refuses a crime, a low one may not refuse a crime (§7).
6. One that has the Speaking function may not refuse a conversation (§6).

| Answer | Effect on difficulty |
|---|---|
| Eager | −1 |
| Willing | 0 |
| Reluctant | +1 |
| Refuses | The act is cancelled |

### Second thoughts

1. When a thinking modus mentis's proposal comes to nothing, the same modus mentis may take it up again.
2. How many times depends on its **readiness to doubt** ([Chapter II §1](02-memory.md#1-what-a-modus-mentis-is)): never at low, once at medium, twice at high.
3. A proposal comes to nothing when the acting modus mentis refuses it, or when it is doubted (below).
4. Each refusal is shown, and the thinking modus mentis acknowledges it before deliberating again.
5. A refused goal is taken up again at once if another acting modus mentis not yet tried for it is one no rule of choice forbids. For a conversation, this must be a speaking one, where any is held.
6. The act is then carried out by one of those, which is not asked whether it would rather not (as in §4), and the thinking ends on an act.
7. If no such acting modus mentis exists, the goal is struck off and the thinking modus mentis deliberates on another.
8. When nothing remains, the thinking ends and says so.
9. Only a refusal given before an act is offered leads to new deliberation. The rules of action (§6) judge the act when it is attempted; a witness in sight or an enemy in the area is a reason the attempt fails, not a reason to think again.
10. The whole chain is one deliberation and costs 1 noetic point.

### Doubt

1. A thinking modus mentis of medium or high readiness to doubt may be doubted; one of low readiness never is.
2. While a further deliberation remains, the reasoning ends on a question: hold to the proposal or not. It is answered before the acting modus mentis is asked.
3. Not holding to it strikes off the goal itself, for every acting modus mentis at once. The doubt is about the end, not the means.
4. The thinking modus mentis then deliberates again on another goal.
5. Either answer is given in the thinking modus mentis's voice.
6. Not holding to it uses up a further deliberation, as a refusal does; holding to it uses none.
7. No doubt is offered on the last deliberation, which would otherwise end the thinking on nothing.

### Reconsideration

1. A waiting act may be thought over again by a thinking modus mentis the player chooses, for 1 noetic point.
2. That modus mentis may hold to the act or reject it, but proposes no other.
3. A will holds to an act of its own course and to no other.
4. Holding to it, it gives the act to an acting modus mentis other than the one that offered it.
5. The new one must be one that no rule of choice forbids and no rule of action (§6) would refuse when the act is attempted. So an act that a witness in sight or an enemy in the area would forbid passes to a discreet modus mentis, where one is held.
6. The new acting modus mentis does not decline it.
7. The new act replaces the old. The old act stays in the record and may no longer be attempted.
8. If the thinking modus mentis rejects the act, or no other acting modus mentis could carry it out, the act offered before stands unchanged.
9. Reconsideration is not offered in the two opening scenes of a run, whose one act has one means.

### The die pool

1. The **die pool** is the sum of the levels of every modus mentis in the chain: the one that observed, the one that deliberated, and the one that acts.
2. The full level of any implement combined with the act is added (see [Chapter IV §7](04-items.md#7-implements)).
3. The pool is never fewer than 1 die.
4. The level counted is the level the body can currently carry, not the level once learned. A wound lowers the ceiling of the modi mentis its organ feeds, and the level is capped at that ceiling ([Chapter II §3](02-memory.md#3-level-and-maximum-level)).
5. An injured body therefore rolls fewer dice.
6. A modus mentis whose capped level is 0 or below is **broken**: it adds no dice and cannot be used at all (§6).

### Difficulty

1. The **difficulty** is the number of sixes required, from 0 to 10.
2. It is the difficulty of that manner of acting on that thing, adjusted by the acting modus mentis's degree of assent (−1 eager, +1 reluctant).
3. Acts of difficulty 0 are not rolled: they cast no dice and succeed. They never fail and never draw the consequences of failure (§8).
4. Some acts are difficulty 0 by nature: opening a door that is not fastened, going up or down a stair, sitting to let the hour pass, and going over to a person to speak.
5. Going over to speak includes introducing oneself, proposing a trade, asking for work, the use of a workshop or an introduction, or simply talking. What is at stake there lies in the conversation that follows ([Chapter VII](07-social.md)).
6. An act of difficulty 1 carried out by an eager modus mentis falls to 0 and is not rolled either.
7. A reluctant modus mentis raises a difficulty-0 act to 1, and it is rolled like any other.
8. A crime is never below difficulty 1: an act that is unlawful where it is done and on whom (§7) has difficulty 1 at least, whatever its manner and however eager the acting modus mentis.
9. Reason for rule 8: what a crime risks is a failure that is overheard, and an act that cannot fail could never be overheard.

### The roll

1. Each die is rolled separately. The act succeeds when the number of sixes reaches the difficulty.
2. Nothing else enters the roll. Organs do not change it; they set the ceilings that let a modus mentis be raised.
3. Humors may change dice after they are rolled ([Chapter III §4](03-humors.md#4-transmuting-virtues)), and the sixes are counted again afterwards.
4. An act whose difficulty is greater than the pool cannot succeed at all. It needs an implement, higher-level modi mentis, or a different chain.
5. Every modus mentis in a chain that carried a successful act gains 1 point of experience.
6. Most acts also teach a modus mentis of their own: the lesson of having done that thing.

**Example.** Three modi mentis at level 1 make a pool of 3 dice. An act of difficulty 4 needs 4
sixes, so it cannot succeed with this chain. With an eager acting modus mentis it becomes difficulty
3, which 3 dice can reach only if every die shows a six.

### Sentiment

1. After an act is resolved, a fourth modus mentis that took no part in it may answer it. This is a modus mentis with the Emotion function, called an **emotion modus mentis**.
2. It answers only when the act's consequences are of a kind that moves one of the emotion modi mentis the body holds.
3. That emotion modus mentis then puts emotion humors into the spleen ([Chapter III §8](03-humors.md#8-emotion-humors)).
4. It gives its own account: not of what was done, which the acting modus mentis already told, but of what it meant to the one who did it.
5. The sentiment comes after the consequences and before anything the act set in motion. A body that struck the first blow of a fight is moved by the blow before the fight begins.
6. What an act sets in motion (a fight, a conversation, work at a station, losing the way) begins only after the act's outcome has been reported.
7. Most acts move no emotion modus mentis, so usually nothing is said. A body that holds none is never moved.

### The two opening scenes

A run opens on two scenes before any place: the childhood recollection, then getting up.

1. The childhood recollection begins with the character observing itself. This observation says what the character has lost, the knowledge of who it is, and reveals the fragments of the first memory, as a container reveals its contents (above).
2. Each memory holds a few fragments. A fragment has no name until it is observed, and which fragment is observed is the observing modus mentis's choice, as for any contents.
3. Remembering a fragment is an act of difficulty 0: it is not rolled and always succeeds. It ends that memory and opens the next, whose fragments are revealed by the fragment just remembered.
4. A memory that has ended cannot be returned to. Its other fragments can no longer be observed, deliberated on or remembered, and an act still waiting on one of them is withdrawn. What was written about them can still be read.
5. A will (§6) is not asked to observe or to deliberate in the childhood recollection. Any other observing or thinking modus mentis may be.
6. When getting up, the character itself is one of the things present, and is observed like any other.
7. Observed, it reveals 3 sensations, as a container reveals its contents (above). A sensation has no name until it is observed, and the observing modus mentis chooses which.
8. Only a sensation allows an act, and the act is getting up. The other things present can be observed and nothing more.
9. Getting up is always difficulty 1, whatever the acting modus mentis's degree of assent. A failure has no penalty, and the act can be decided on again.
10. Nothing in either scene costs a noetic point (§3).

## 6. Rules of action and rules of choice

Two separate sets of rules stand between a body and an act. **Rules of action** stop an act that
was chosen; **rules of choice** keep an act from being offered in the first place.

### Rules of action

1. A rule of action judges an act already chosen, at the moment it is attempted.
2. It **rejects** the act and gives the reason in the acting modus mentis's own voice. The attempt's **1 noetic point stays spent** (§3).
3. These rules are about circumstance: someone is watching, the door needs a key, there is nowhere left to put a log.
4. The rules are checked in the order of the table, and the first that applies gives the refusal.
5. The broken-modus-mentis rule comes first because it alone is about the body, not the circumstance. A character told that a witness is watching will try another way; one told the arm is ruined will not. Its refusal names the failed parts and the wounds behind them.
6. Under the missing-implement rule the act stays standing. The refusal says what was wanted, that bare hands cannot do it, and which implement is missing.
7. That act may then be taken up again only with an implement combined, never bare-handed a second time.
8. Only when nothing combinable is carried is it closed like any other refused act.

| Order | Rule | Refuses when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Broken modus mentis | The acting modus mentis's capped level is 0 or below (§5). |
| 2 | Damaged tongue | A conversation, when the speech fluency value (the tongue score, 0–5; [Chapter I §6](01-anatomy.md#6-derived-values)) is 0, or every speaking modus mentis held is broken. |
| 3 | Poor affinity | A conversation with an enemy, unless it is to reconcile or appease; or with someone who finds the character insufferable, unless it is to reconcile. |
| 4 | Principle | A crime, when the acting modus mentis has high moral level. This is a safety net: the rule of choice below normally refuses it earlier. |
| 5 | Witness in sight | A crime, with a witness in the same area and an acting modus mentis that is not discreet (§7). |
| 6 | Under threat | Any act except facing the enemy, while an enemy is in the area and the acting modus mentis is not discreet (§7). |
| 7 | Confronted | Any act except answering the confronting witness, when the acting modus mentis is not discreet (§7). |
| 8 | Missing implement | The act needs an implement and none is combined. |
| 9 | Nothing to strike with | A blow, with nothing left to strike it with. |
| 10 | Carrying capacity | Taking something when the body can carry no more. |

### Broken modi mentis

1. A broken modus mentis is still offered for observing, deliberating and acting, and then refused. It is not hidden.
2. Reason for rule 1: the refusal is what tells the player about the injury. Removing it silently would take modi mentis away with no account of where they went.
3. Speech is the exception. A reply in a conversation is written as a thing said, with no room for a refusal, so a broken speaking modus mentis is passed over instead.
4. When the last speaking modus mentis is passed over, the damaged-tongue rule answers for it. It asks two questions: whether there is a voice, and whether any modus mentis is left able to use it.

### Rules of choice

1. A rule of choice narrows what is *offered*.
2. It **withholds** silently and **costs nothing**, because nothing was refused.
3. These rules are about character: "that never occurred to me".
4. Practical difference: an act blocked by a rule of action was attempted and stopped, and the attempt is paid for; an act withheld by a rule of choice was never attempted.

### The senses need an observing modus mentis

1. In a body of the human anatomy, a thinking modus mentis without the Observation function is never offered any of the four senses as a goal: to examine, to contemplate, to listen or to smell.
2. A beast is not under rule 1: each of its thinking modi mentis is offered the senses, which are most of what a beast can do.
3. On the acting side the rule is narrower and holds for every anatomy: an acting modus mentis without the Observation function, chosen to carry out an examination, refuses at once without being asked. The refusal says it would act rather than stand and observe.
4. The other three senses are carried out by whichever acting modus mentis is chosen.

### Principle refuses a crime early

1. An acting modus mentis of high moral level chosen to carry out a crime refuses at once, without being asked, while the act is still being deliberated.
2. No act is offered, and no point is spent on an attempt.
3. The refusal is recorded like any other (§4): the same thinking modus mentis is not offered that goal again, and another that chooses it is offered as means only those that would not refuse.

### Speech turns toward people

A modus mentis with the Speaking function puts conversation first, at every step of the chain it
takes part in.

1. Observing, it does not decline (§5) when the thing it is asked to observe is a person.
2. Thinking, when the observed thing allows any conversation, only the conversations are offered.
3. Exception to rule 2: a low-moral thinking modus mentis already narrowed to crimes (§7) keeps the crimes, because its moral level is applied first.
4. When the goal is a conversation, only the acting modi mentis that speak are offered as means, if any is held.
5. A speaking acting modus mentis cannot refuse a conversation, though it may be reluctant.
6. A rule of action that forbids the conversation itself (a hostile listener, a tongue with nothing left to say) still applies.
7. Where nobody can be spoken to, none of this narrows anything.

### A will follows its own course

A **will** is a modus mentis that wants a few particular kinds of act (see
[Chapter II §7](02-memory.md#7-what-the-modi-mentis-are-for)). Its **course** is the shortest series
of acts from where the body stands to an act of a kind it wants.

1. At every step of the chain, a will attends only to the first act of such a course. If several courses are equally short, any of their first acts will do.
2. In what it says, a person on its course is named the way the narration names them elsewhere.
3. Asked to observe a named thing, it does so if the thing allows that first act. Otherwise it declines, naming the act that would begin the course.
4. Asked to observe one of a kind, it observes the first thing of that kind that allows that first act, and none where none does.
5. Deliberating, it is less strict. Its candidates are the acts the thing allows that begin a course to what it wants of at most 3 acts, the wanted act included, or of no more acts than the shortest course the place offers, however long.
6. It is offered only the candidate with the shortest course. Where several courses are equally short, it chooses among their first acts itself.
7. An act already waiting is not a candidate (§4). So a second deliberation on the same thing takes the next course through it, within the same limits. With none left, the will proposes nothing and lets the thing go.
8. On a thing that allows no candidate, it proposes nothing and says only what it wants.
9. A will is never doubted, because its goal is set by its course.
10. A will is of medium readiness, so it tries again once, and only with another means for an act on its way.
11. In both observing and deliberating, it gives its course as its reason.
12. Where no course exists within the place, it declines in both and says that only leaving offers what it wants.
13. A will that has the Action function is its own means for the act it wants and is not asked how willingly: it is eager, and the difficulty falls by 1.
14. An act on its way is carried out by whichever means is chosen for it, like any goal.
15. A will cannot refuse an act that would be its candidate. A rule of action, which judges circumstance and not character, still can.

### A visit made for one want

1. From the world map, a will may be sent to look for one of its wants among the places in range.
2. The places are examined in order of the cost of the road to each, nearest first.
3. The first place in which a course to that want exists from the place's entrance, at the hour of arrival, is proposed as the destination.
4. A visit reached by that journey is made for that want. While it lasts, the will works out its course to that want alone, however long: the course it observes by, and the things it will observe.
5. Deliberating, it first takes a course through the thing to that want, whatever its length.
6. Only where the thing allows no such course are its other wants candidates, and then only by a course of at most 3 acts.
7. Where no course to the wanted act remains in the place (the thing is done, or gone), the will works from all its wants again.
8. Any other visit, including a later visit to the same place, follows the rules of the previous subsection.

## 7. Crime and being seen

Whether an act is a crime depends on where it is done and to whom, and what a crime costs depends on
who sees or hears it.

### What makes a crime

1. No manner of acting is a crime in itself.
2. Standing in someone's private area makes *anything* done there a **trespass**.
3. This is never hidden: throughout a visit, the narration states whether the ground underfoot is private.
4. Private ground teaches no routine (see [Chapter IX](09-routines.md)).
5. Beyond trespass, each act that can be a crime has its own condition.
6. Striking someone who already counts the character an enemy is not a crime: the quarrel was declared before the blow.
7. Forcing the lock of a public storehouse is not a crime: it is nobody's private property.

### The four tenures

| Tenure | Where | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Private | Somebody's house, or any room that is somebody's, including the rooms off a large building's passages | Every act is a trespass. Taking anything is theft, whoever let the character in. |
| Protected | Every room that is not private within a building whose entrance stands open by day and is shut at night: the hall of a workshop, shop or inn; the passages and public rooms of a temple or castle | Standing there is no trespass. Acts that lay hands on the place are crimes until a master of the building gives leave. |
| Public | The streets, yards and fields of a farm, village or city | Nobody's private space; only acts with a crime condition of their own are crimes. |
| Wild | The open ground of a forest, plain, cave, coast, mountain, peak and the climate biomes | As public, and striking or killing a beast here is hunting, not a crime. |

**Private ground**

1. Private ground is never entered from other ground by accident.
2. The only ways in are a door locked against the character (the door of a house, or of any room that is somebody's) and ways that are themselves an intrusion: a window slipped through, a wall climbed.
3. No open way, stair or unlocked door leads from other ground into private ground.
4. Within private ground, the ways between rooms are open.

**Protected ground**

1. An act that leaves the place as it was is no crime: speech, the senses, waiting, walking.
2. An act that lays hands on the place is a crime until a living master of the building has given leave: taking what lies there, working its stations, cutting, extracting and breaking.
3. With leave, these acts are lawful, and leave once given is kept.
4. Leave can be asked only by a body holding a trade whose station stands in that building ([Chapter X §4](10-crafting.md)). Anywhere else, what lies on such a floor must be bought or stolen.
5. A floor whose master is dead or mute needs nobody's leave.
6. A roof is outside the door, and is public.

**Public and wild ground**

1. A store kept on wild ground (a woodshed, a net shed, an ore store) is somebody's stock, and its tenure is public.
2. Tenure also decides whether a beast belongs to anybody.
3. Striking or killing a beast that is not already an enemy is a crime on any ground except wild, because the beast is taken to be somebody's. On wild ground it is hunting.
4. A creature small enough to be taken in the hand is caught or crushed rather than struck. Neither is a crime against it on any ground, though on private ground it is still a trespass.
5. A person is never game: every crime against a person is a crime on wild ground too.

### Moral level

Each modus mentis has a **moral level**: low, medium or high
([Chapter II §1](02-memory.md#1-what-a-modus-mentis-is)). Low and high each change the behaviour of
both the thinking and the acting modus mentis.

| | A thinking modus mentis | An acting modus mentis |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Crimes are removed from its list of goals. Removing every goal is a valid result. | Refuses a crime at once, unasked, before the act is offered. |
| **Low** | If any goal on offer is a crime, only the crimes are offered, and it may not decline them all: it chooses one. | Cannot refuse: it is not given the option to decline. |

1. Medium moral level changes nothing.
2. Both columns are needed because the goal and the means are two separate choices: a principled modus mentis may still be chosen to carry out a goal another modus mentis chose.

### Being seen: two questions

Whether the character can act, and what a failure costs, are judged separately. Keeping them apart is
what gives discretion its value. A **discreet** modus mentis is one that acts quietly
([Chapter II §1](02-memory.md#1-what-a-modus-mentis-is)).

Proximity has three levels: **Visual** (in the same area), **Audio** (in the same **section**: the
same building, or the same stretch of open ground; a door is a gate, not a wall) and **None**.

**May the character act at all?**

1. This is judged on raw proximity by the rules of action (§6).
2. Someone in the same area blocks a crime by a modus mentis that is not discreet.
3. A discreet modus mentis may always attempt. That permission is all that discretion buys at close range.
4. The acts that answer a confronting witness (below) are exempt: done to the one already watching, they leave nobody else to be caught by.
5. A discreet modus mentis may not decline an act that only its discretion permits here: a crime in sight of a witness, any act other than facing the threat while an enemy is in the area, any act other than answering a confronting witness.
6. It may be reluctant to do such an act, but may not refuse it, unless principle refuses it first (§6).
7. Out of everybody's sight, it declines like any other modus mentis.

**What does failing cost?**

1. This is judged on **effective proximity**, which applies discretion.
2. Discretion silences a body through a wall, never in the same room.
3. For a discreet modus mentis, Visual stays Visual, and Audio becomes None. For any other, effective proximity equals raw proximity.

| Effective proximity | A witness | An enemy |
|---|---|---|
| Visual | Caught in the act: a confrontation, which is a conversation; failing it starts a fight. | They attack, on their own initiative. |
| Audio | They come to look: they move into the area. | The same. |
| None | Nothing. | Nothing. |

1. The Audio level is an approach, not a verdict. Nothing is said yet. The cost is that someone now stands in the area, having come because of the character, and that is a confrontation (below).
2. Being heard is announced as a consequence of the failed act.
3. The one who came enters the record of every party member in that area, already observed (§4): an enemy as the one who came looking, a witness as the one who came to see what the noise was.

### Under threat, and confronted

Two kinds of presence in the area restrict the character, and they work alike on purpose.

1. An **enemy** in the area permits only the acts that answer them: to strike, to kill, to appease, to reconcile, to tame.
2. Any act that fails in front of an enemy starts a fight, with the enemy striking first.
3. A **confronting witness** is someone in the area with a reason to stop the character: either they came because they heard a crime, or the ground is private and every act there is a trespass they see.
4. A person merely standing in a public place confronts nobody.
5. A confronting witness permits only striking them, killing them, or going up to them to explain oneself.
6. Any act that fails in front of a confronting witness, lawful or not, is caught red-handed: the confrontation opens, worded after the crime they heard, or as trespass if they heard none.
7. Explaining oneself opens that same confrontation by choice. Every check in it is then 1 six easier than when caught, but never easier than 1 six. Someone who comes forward is half believed.
8. Failing the approach is itself a slip in front of the witness, and the character is caught after all.
9. In both cases, a discreet modus mentis may still attempt any act. It does not buy safety: a discreet act that fails in the same area is seen.

### While confronted

1. While an enemy or a confronting witness stands over a character, that character cannot walk away. It can observe and deliberate only on what stands in the area where it is.
2. The one confronting it is observed like anything else, for 1 noetic point (§3), before it can be deliberated on. An observation of their kind in that area is of them first.
3. A thinking modus mentis is offered only the acts the presence permits (above). This is a rule of choice: the other acts are withheld silently, and cost nothing. It does not apply while the character holds a discreet acting modus mentis, which alone could attempt them.
4. A thing that affords none of the acts still offered cannot be deliberated on while the confrontation lasts. It can still be observed.
5. The character cannot speak to a companion (§10).
6. Acts it had already decided on, in the area where it stands, still wait and can still be attempted, each for 1 noetic point (§3). They are judged by the rules above.
7. Someone asleep confronts nobody and witnesses nothing.
8. Party members standing in other areas are not confronted. They do nothing while the confrontation lasts (§10).

### Leaving

1. A place is left the way it was entered: by its one entrance, which lies beyond the area the place was entered by.
2. Leaving means walking there: from the current area, back along known ways, to that area and out.
3. Every party member leaves, each from where it stands. Members standing together go together.
4. The departure is a walk like any other (§1): a member who would be confronted on the way is stopped there. The party is then split between those already out and those still inside.

### Running away

1. While an enemy or a confronting witness is in the area, leaving becomes a **runaway**.
2. A runaway is one roll of dice equal to the runaway dice value (the feet score, minimum 1; [Chapter I §6](01-anatomy.md#6-derived-values)), needing 1 six. Humors may change the dice as for any roll.
3. Failure starts the fight with an enemy, or the confrontation with a witness.
4. Success sets the body walking for the entrance, and the walk is checked the rest of the way out.
5. Every further area it passes through, including the areas a path runs past, is judged as if the body stood in it.
6. Wherever an enemy or a witness would confront it, it is stopped and rolls again on the same terms.
7. Each success carries it on to the next such area, or out of the place when none is left.
8. A failure at such a stop forces nothing: the body stays in the area where it was stopped, and noetic points are not restored.
9. Whoever stopped it enters its record (§4) and stands in the area with it, so leaving is a runaway again.
10. A confrontation ends with the conversation it opens. A witness who came because of the character then returns to what they were doing, unless the conversation made them an enemy.

### Found waiting

1. A householder who comes home at nightfall to find the character waiting in their house (see [Chapter V §8](05-travel.md#8-the-periods-of-the-day)) confronts the character like any witness.
2. The same three ways open the conversation: a failed act in front of them, a failed runaway, or going up to explain oneself.
3. This conversation is not the one a crime opens. It asks why the character is still there and whether they are to be kept for the night.
4. Every rolled check in it uses the hardest difficulty ladder ([Chapter VII §2](07-social.md#2-the-conversation)).
5. Winning it means being allowed to stay. For the rest of the visit, standing anywhere in that building is no trespass, and nobody in it confronts the character for being there.
6. Losing it means being shown the door, which ends the visit.
7. The leave covers presence only. The rooms are still somebody's: taking from them is still theft, and forcing a locked door in them is still a crime, though an unlocked door may be opened.
8. No routine is learned there, and another building is another house.
9. An act that is a crime of its own and fails in front of the householder opens the confrontation for that crime, not this one.

### After the visit

1. Enmity lasts across visits. Walking away from a fight ends the fight, not the quarrel.
2. A crime costs what it costs at the time, plus what the person who saw it thinks of the character afterwards.
3. Nothing is written down, and no authority pursues the character beyond the room.

## 8. Failure

A failed act costs three things, in this order.

1. **The budget**: a failure does not refill the noetic points (§3).
2. **A social consequence**, read from effective proximity (§7): at most one of caught, attacked, or approached. These are three levels of one scale, not separate effects.
3. **A penalty**, chosen at random from what that manner of acting risks. Every failure has one.

### The four penalties

| Penalty | Applies to | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| A wound | Attempts that can hurt the one making them | A wound, dated with the day so that it can heal. |
| The way lost | Acts that find a way: walking, following a path or a quarry, setting out for a distant view | The visit ends with the act; the party stands again in open country, outside the place. |
| The hour wasted | Hard and slow acts: a climb, a swim, a crossing, a labour | The period of the day advances by 1, with everything that follows from that. |
| The thread lost | Acts that risk nothing else: the senses, speech, waiting | 1 noetic point lost beyond the one already spent. Since a failure does not refill the budget, it stays lost until an act succeeds. |

### Penalties that cannot apply

1. A penalty that cannot apply is replaced by another drawn from the act's remaining risks.
2. The way cannot be lost if someone saw or heard the failure. The one who caught the character does not let them wander off, and the one who heard comes to the area the character is still in.
3. The hour cannot be wasted if the failure was seen, because it then starts a fight or a confrontation there and then. A failure that was only heard can still waste the hour.
4. The thread cannot be lost with no noetic point left.
5. A wound must be one the body can have. A generic graze becomes the acting body's own graze of the same kind; a named injury to a part the body lacks is replaced.

### A missed blow

1. A blow aimed at someone is the exception to all of the above.
2. A swing that misses starts the fight anyway, and the social consequence and penalty are not applied. A quarrel the character started outweighs any cost of clumsiness.
3. What the miss costs is the initiative. See [Chapter VIII §5](08-fighting.md#5-the-opening-blow).

## 9. What a place remembers

Because a scene is rebuilt identically on every arrival (§1), only what is stored explicitly
survives a visit.

**Rules**

1. Four things are stored, listed in the table.
2. Corpses, wounds dealt and forced doors are not stored. They last for one visit only.
3. Departure is permanent. Reason: a replacement generated from the same seed would be the same person who left, so the place stays emptier.

| Stored | What it records |
|---|---|
| Affinity | What each person here thinks of the character. |
| Enmity | Who here counts the character an enemy. This is separate from affinity and changes independently of it. |
| Depletion | Which gathering spots have been stripped, and when. |
| The departed | Everyone this place has lost for good. |

## 10. The party in a place

Each member of a party is a separate body in the place, with its own position, its own budget and
its own record.

**Rules**

1. All members enter by the same area. After that each stands where it was last moved, and a move moves only the member who made it.
2. Each member observes, deliberates and acts for itself. It spends its own noetic points (§3), uses its own modi mentis, and keeps its own record of what it has observed (§4).
3. Which member is played can be changed at any moment, at no cost, except in the 2 cases below.
4. A member with no noetic point left cannot be played (§3).
5. While an enemy or a confronting witness confronts any member (§7), only the confronted members can be played. The others cannot observe, deliberate, act, walk or leave until the confrontation ends.
6. The time of day is shared: it is the same hour for every member.
7. Every check of what a body can do consults the acting body, not the protagonist ([Chapter I §5](01-anatomy.md#5-capabilities)).
8. A beast is offered no conversation, no lock, no implement and no letters: about half of everything there is to do.
9. Tracking, scenting, going places and fighting remain open to a beast.
10. A conversation with the people of a place belongs to the protagonist. No companion opens one: its replies, the affinity it earns and what it settles are the protagonist's.
11. Two acts consult the protagonist whoever performs them, because they concern the party itself: taming a beast, and proposing that someone join.

### Speaking to a companion

1. A member with a speaking modus mentis may speak of a thing it has observed to a companion standing in the same area, or in an area joined to it by an open way or by a connector other than a view.
2. It costs the speaker 1 noetic point.
3. What was said is recorded under the thing in the speaker's record.
4. The thing enters the listener's record as observed, with those words in place of an observation of its own. The listener deliberates on it from what it was told.
5. The thing is also known to the listener from then on (§4), on later visits as on this one. Reason: a thing one member observed is otherwise unknown to every other member.
6. Speaking does not change which member is played.

### Fights and changes to the party

1. A fight involves the members standing in the area where it breaks out, and nobody else.
2. Whoever is fought counts those members enemies afterwards.
3. The protagonist may be absent from a fight.
4. A fight lost with the protagonist in it ends the run ([Chapter VIII](08-fighting.md)). A fight lost by companions alone kills them, and the protagonist goes on.
5. Someone who joins during a visit stands where the member who won them stands, having observed nothing.
6. A member who dies leaves a corpse where it stood (§11).

## 11. What a killing leaves

A death leaves a **corpse**: an ordinary point of interest in the area where the death happened, not
a place to enter.

### Parts

1. A corpse's contents are its harvestable parts, offered as the corpse's own acts. One observation of the body offers everything that can be cut from it.
2. Identical parts form a single goal, and each cutting removes one.
3. A body yields between 4 and 8 parts.
4. Reason for rule 3: fewer than 4 does not repay the cost of getting them (a killing, a blade, and a noetic point for every attempt). More than 8 is more than one observation can offer and more than can be carried away.
5. Duplicates count toward the 8 but not toward the goals. A body with 3 cuts of meat offers one cutting act that can be done 3 times.
6. Creatures differ in which parts and how many of each, never in what the parts are called. The same flesh and bone come from every body; a bear gives 8 where a smaller beast gives 4.
7. A creature small enough to be taken in the hand, rather than killed, yields 2 or 3 parts, all given at once when it is taken.
8. Crushing such a creature leaves nothing.

### Humans and companions

1. A human is opened like any other body: the same flesh, plus hair from the head and the skull beneath it.
2. A human also leaves a separate container of what they carried.
3. Everything in a corpse is flesh: cutting acts take it, seizing acts refuse it. Everything in the belongings beside it is cloth and steel, and the reverse holds.
4. A fallen companion leaves the same, by species and by what they carried. Having been in the party makes no difference.
5. What is cut from a companion who was a beast is what would be cut from any beast of that kind.

### Blades

1. Cutting requires a blade combined with the act ([Chapter IV §7](04-items.md#7-implements)).
2. So does killing, when it yields a body without a fight.
3. A body without hands to hold a blade is never offered either act, rather than being offered them and refused.

### When a corpse appears

1. A corpse needs no observation. It enters the record of every party member standing in the area, already observed (§4), and can be deliberated on at once.
2. Several bodies enter in the order they fell.
3. No modus mentis observed it, so a roll made on it counts only the modi mentis that deliberate and act.
