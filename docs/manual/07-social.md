# VII. Of Social Intercourse

Dealings with persons run upon a single ladder of regard, and are settled by a single check. Barter
and hire are conveniences opened by that check rather than systems standing beside it.

## 1. The ladder of affinity

Each person holds an opinion of each party member, upon a ladder of six rungs and one landing beside
it. **The rung's ordinal is the bonus dice it grants in conversation.**

| Rung | Dice |
|---|---|
| Stranger | 0 |
| Annoying acquaintance | 1 |
| Distant acquaintance | 2 |
| Acquaintance | 3 |
| Distant friend | 4 |
| Close friend | 5 |
| **Suspicious** | **0** |

**Suspicious stands off the ladder.** It is the state of one who has been talked down out of
hostility but has not forgiven it, and it grants nothing. Its neighbours are named expressly: a step
upward from it reaches distant acquaintance, a step downward annoying acquaintance. It is escapable
in both directions — a dead end there would mean that a person reconciled with could never
afterwards be improved.

Three further properties of the ladder:

- A movement is a **named step**, never an increment upon the number. A step down from stranger
  clamps at annoying acquaintance; there is no rung below.
- **A movement of nought does not move the ladder at all.** A conversation that left matters as they
  stood must not be read as a step downward.
- Regard is **not** the same thing as enmity. A person may count you an enemy and hold a rung upon
  the ladder besides, and the two move independently: reconciliation clears the enmity and leaves
  you merely suspect.

Both facts survive between visits, as [Chapter VI §9](06-narration.md#9-what-a-place-remembers)
describes.

## 2. The conversation

A conversation is a tree. The other party speaks; a set of replies is offered; the reply chosen
leads to another line, and so on, until the branch reaches its **resolution** — the single dice
check that settles the whole exchange.

**How many replies are offered** is the **tongue's** measure of fluency, from nought to five, and
never more than the count of speaking modi mentis the body can bring to bear. A tongue at nought — or
disabled by a High wound — offers none, and such a body cannot hold a conversation at all.

**A speaking modus mentis the frame can no longer carry is passed over** rather than refused
([Chapter II §3](02-memory.md#3-level-and-its-ceiling)). Elsewhere such a manner of the mind is
offered and declines aloud, but a reply is set down as a thing said, and a refusal has no room in it.
When wounds have taken the last of them, the conversation is not opened at all: the rule that governs
a damaged tongue answers for the want of a mind to use it, and says which parts of the frame failed.

**A branch is between two and four replies deep.** Nothing shorter is a conversation; nothing longer
is worth the reading.

### The pool

The pool assembled at the resolution is the sum of four things, confined to the range one to
fifteen:

| Contribution | From |
|---|---|
| **Regard** | The rung of the ladder, as above. |
| **Eloquence** | The summed levels of the modi mentis behind every reply chosen along the branch. |
| **Attire** | One die per worn garment that flatters *this person's* social standing. |
| **Countenance** | The **visage's** measure — one die per five points of the region. |

Two of these are worth distinguishing. **Attire is particular**: the coat that helps with a
craftsman does nothing for a hermit, it must be actually worn, and it stops accumulating at six dice
— past that one is already as well turned out as a standing can read. **Countenance is general**: a
fair face owes nothing to who is listening, and nothing can be done about it after composition. The
eight standings, and what dress speaks to each, are in [Chapter IV §9](04-items.md#9-garments).

### The difficulty

The difficulty is not authored freely. Because the pool grows with every reply, a fixed difficulty
would make a long branch nearly free; the difficulty therefore rises with depth, but slightly more
slowly than the pool does, so that committing to a longer exchange remains modestly the better play
rather than being a pure tax.

Two ladders exist:

| Depth in replies | Ordinary matters | Matters of consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 six | 2 sixes |
| 3 | 2 sixes | 2 sixes |
| 4 | 2 sixes | 3 sixes |

The first governs small talk and the opening of a trade; the second reconciliation with an enemy,
the asking of work, talking one's way out of a crime, and the graver of the conversations a calling
opens — confession, petition, a bribe, and the asking to be taught. A difficulty exceeding the pool is lowered
to it, so no resolution is unreachable.

The humors may be spent upon this roll as upon any other, to the **viscera's** limit.

Certain resolutions are **forced** and roll nothing: the branch of a confrontation in which one
provokes the confronter always ends in a fight.

**A conversation that carries teaches three things, not one.** The first is the general skill of
walking up to somebody and opening one's mouth, which is the same whatever follows and is what the
act itself teaches. The second is the conversation's own lesson, particular to the business
attempted: to have begged a coin successfully is beggary, whatever the act of addressing a stranger
may be. The third is drawn from the branch that was actually walked and from the person who was
listening — a matter pressed upon a man of standing is not the matter pressed upon a labourer, and
does not teach the same thing. Where no lesson has been set upon a branch, the second stands alone.

A settled conversation, like a settled act, may then be answered by a disposition the body holds —
alms taken, an enmity let go, an introduction granted or an exchange that came to nothing are all
consequences a disposition may be moved by. The rule is the same one, and is given in [Chapter III
§8](03-humors.md#8-the-humors-of-the-passions).

## 3. Introduction, and what it gates

**The introduction is the gate for conversation.** Presenting oneself is what turns a stranger into
a distant acquaintance, and nearly everything else stands behind it: barter, hire, the deepening of
a relation, the gathering of knowledge, and the asking of a third party to vouch for you — the last
because asking one who does not know you to vouch for you cannot work in that order.

Two acts opt out, and for reasons of their own. **Begging** and **provocation** are each *about*
addressing one who does not know you: behind an introduction, begging would leave the destitute with
nothing to say to anyone, and provocation would be unreachable against exactly the people worth
using it upon.

**Some conversations belong to a calling.** A blessing or a confession can be had only of the
religious; a petition only of a lord, a steward or a captain; talk of soldiering only with a guard or
a captain; talk of far places only with a merchant or a sailor; a bribe only offered to a guard, a
clerk, a steward or a captain; teaching asked only of a scholar, a clerk or a monk; a song joined
only where someone sings at their work; work commissioned only of a cobbler, a tailor, a potter, a
chandler or a tanner; a remedy asked only of an apothecary or a barber; alms given only to a beggar;
and the gossip of the town heard only from those whose labour takes them into every house — a
laundress, a water-carrier, a porter or a barber. Who the person is decides whether the matter can be
raised at all — the rung of the ladder only how well it goes. Five of these opt out of the
introduction as begging does, because each is by its nature addressed to strangers: a blessing is
not refused for not having been introduced, a petition is the way an unknown party reaches authority,
a bribe is offered to precisely the official who does not know you, a remedy is not withheld from the
sick for being unknown, and alms are given to whoever holds out the bowl. Each such conversation teaches by its branch,
so that what is learned depends on how the matter was put as much as on whether it carried; a refused
confession, petition or bribe costs a step of regard, while any of the others refused costs
nothing.

## 4. Barter

A conversation may open a merchant's catalogue. What a person stocks follows from what they are: a
catalogue is assembled from the kinds of goods that trade deals in, so not everything that exists is
something somebody sells.

**Not every person trades, and none trades everywhere.** Those who keep a craft, a shop, a store or
a house of business trade; soldiers, nobles, clergy, scholars and the labouring poor of the streets
do not, and a great building trades through the one of its people charged with its stores. A
person's goods are not carried upon them: they are kept on their **premises** — the counter of a
workshop or an inn, the barn or shed of a farm, the store of a stable or a fold, a woodcutter's or a
fisherman's shed, the entrance hall of a great building — and a trade may be proposed only while
both parties stand there. The same person met elsewhere will talk, but will not deal.

Every trader comes to their premises at least once in the day. A shop counter, and the store of a
farm or a stock-keeping place, is kept by somebody able to trade at every period between dawn and
evening; a solitary worker of the wild keeps their shed for a single period only, and must be met
there then.

Prices stand in one denomination each, and **there is no conversion between denominations**: a
purchase must be met with coins of the item's own kind. A merchant's price is the item's reference
price with a small variation upon it, confined to the range one to a hundred.

A catalogue line may deliver more than one item, and may name itself otherwise than the inventory
does — a bottle of ale being, in the pack, a bottle and an ale.

## 5. Hire

A conversation may also open an offer of **work**. Work is not a task or an errand: it is an
abstract exchange of the body's **time** for coin and for experience.

**Pay** is stated as the days that must be worked to earn one coin of the job's denomination, so
that the poorly-paid trades stay legible without fractions — a labourer earning one silver every
hundred and twenty days. The coin earned for a stint is the whole part of the days divided by that
rate.

**Every job trains exactly three modi mentis**, listed most defining first, and the experience is
front-loaded upon the first:

| Rank | Days per point of experience |
|---|---|
| First | 30 |
| Second | 90 |
| Third | 150 |

A modus mentis already held accrues that experience, advancing in level as the **pineal gland's**
threshold is crossed, and stopping at its ceiling. A modus mentis **not** held is learned at the
first level **only if the stint's experience for it reaches the threshold in one go** — otherwise
the stint teaches it nothing at all. A modus mentis so learned is lodged by the ordinary procedure
of [Chapter II §6](02-memory.md#6-acquisition-filing-and-loss), and may therefore displace another.

**Work advances the calendar** by the days worked. It is, with the completion of a journey, one of
the only two things that does.
