# VII. Social Interaction

Dealings with people use one scale of regard (affinity) and are settled by one dice check per
conversation. Trade and hired work are opened by that check; they are not separate systems.

**In short**

1. Each person holds one of 7 affinity levels toward each party member; the level's number is the bonus dice it adds in conversation.
2. A conversation is a branch of 2 to 4 replies ending in one dice check, which needs a number of sixes set by the branch's depth and stakes.
3. The pool for that check = affinity + the levels of the modi mentis behind the replies + flattering garments (up to 6) + Countenance, kept between 1 and 15.
4. Most requests need an introduction first; trade, begging, provocation and a few others do not.
5. Trade happens only on a trader's premises; hired work trades days for coin and experience.

## 1. Affinity

Affinity is what one person thinks of one party member.

**Rules**

1. Each person holds an affinity level toward each party member.
2. The levels form a ladder of 6 steps, plus **Suspicious**, which stands beside the ladder.
3. A level's number is the bonus dice it adds to a conversation check (§2).
4. Suspicious adds 0 dice. It is the state of someone talked out of hostility who has not forgiven it.
5. One step up from Suspicious reaches Distant acquaintance; one step down reaches Annoying acquaintance.
6. Reason for rule 5: if Suspicious were a dead end, a person reconciled with could never be improved afterwards.
7. Affinity moves by whole named steps, never by adding to the number.
8. A step down from Stranger stops at Annoying acquaintance; there is no level below it.
9. A change of 0 does not move affinity at all; a conversation that left things as they were is not a step down.
10. Affinity is separate from enmity (being counted an enemy, see [Chapter VI §7](06-narration.md#7-crime-and-being-seen)).
11. A person can count a party member an enemy and hold an affinity level toward them at the same time; the two move separately.
12. Reconciliation clears the enmity and leaves the person Suspicious.
13. Both affinity and enmity are kept between visits ([Chapter VI §9](06-narration.md#9-what-a-place-remembers)).

| Level | Bonus dice |
|---|---|
| Stranger | 0 |
| Annoying acquaintance | 1 |
| Distant acquaintance | 2 |
| Acquaintance | 3 |
| Distant friend | 4 |
| Close friend | 5 |
| Suspicious (off the ladder) | 0 |

## 2. The conversation

A conversation is a tree: the other person speaks, the character picks a reply, and each reply leads
to another line until the branch reaches its **resolution**, the one dice check that settles the
whole exchange.

### Replies

Each reply is supplied by a **speaking modus mentis** (a modus mentis with the Speaking function; see
[Chapter II §1](02-memory.md#1-what-a-modus-mentis-is)).

**Rules**

1. The number of replies offered is the **Speech Fluency**, a value derived from the tongue score (see [Chapter I §6](01-anatomy.md#6-derived-values)): 1 reply per point, from 0 to 5.
2. The number of replies offered is never more than the number of speaking modi mentis the character can use.
3. A tongue at 0, or one disabled by a High wound, offers no replies, and the character cannot hold a conversation at all.
4. A speaking modus mentis the body can no longer carry (its maximum level has fallen too low; [Chapter II §3](02-memory.md#3-level-and-maximum-level)) is left out, not offered and refused.
5. Reason for rule 4: elsewhere such a modus mentis is offered and refuses aloud, but a reply is something said, and a refusal does not fit in it.
6. When wounds have taken the last usable speaking modus mentis, the conversation does not open at all; the same rule that covers a damaged tongue reports which parts of the body failed.
7. A branch is 2 to 4 replies deep.

### The dice pool

The pool rolled at the resolution is the sum of four parts, kept between 1 and 15.

| Part | Dice |
|---|---|
| Affinity | the level's bonus dice (§1) |
| Eloquence | the summed levels of the modi mentis behind every reply chosen along the branch |
| Attire | 1 die per worn garment that flatters this person's social standing, up to 6 |
| Countenance | the visage score ÷ 5, rounded down |

**Rules**

1. Attire depends on the listener: a coat that helps with a craftsman does nothing for a hermit.
2. A garment counts for attire only while it is worn.
3. Attire stops at 6 dice; past that the character is already as well dressed as a standing can recognise.
4. The eight social standings, and which garments flatter each, are in [Chapter IV §9](04-items.md#9-garments).
5. **Countenance** is a value derived from the visage score (the summed score of the eyes, ears, nose, tongue and teeth; see [Chapter I §6](01-anatomy.md#6-derived-values)).
6. Countenance applies to every listener, and cannot be changed after character creation.
7. Humors may be spent on this roll as on any other ([Chapter III §4](03-humors.md#4-transmuting-virtues)), up to the viscera score per roll (minimum 1).

| Visage score | 0–4 | 5–9 | 10–14 | 15–19 | 20–22 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Countenance (dice) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |

### The difficulty

The difficulty is the number of sixes the pool must show. It is set by the branch's depth and by one
of four ladders.

**Rules**

1. The pool grows with every reply, so a fixed difficulty would make a long branch almost free.
2. The difficulty therefore rises with depth, but a little more slowly than the pool, so a longer exchange stays slightly the better choice.
3. The Ordinary and Consequential ladders sit 2 sixes higher than the replies alone would put them, because affinity, garments and Countenance also add dice.
4. A difficulty higher than the pool is lowered to the pool's size, so no resolution is impossible; but then every die must show a six.
5. Some resolutions are **forced** and roll nothing.
6. Provoking someone who confronts the character always ends in a fight.
7. Telling a householder that the character will leave always ends at the door.

| Depth (replies) | Formality | Ordinary | Consequential | Grave |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 six | 3 sixes | 4 sixes | 5 sixes |
| 3 | 2 sixes | 4 sixes | 4 sixes | 5 sixes |
| 4 | 2 sixes | 4 sixes | 5 sixes | 6 sixes |

| Ladder | Conversations |
|---|---|
| Formality | an introduction; proposing a trade, buying or selling |
| Ordinary | small talk; deepening a relationship; begging; gathering knowledge; gossip; a commission; a remedy; alms; a blessing; talk of soldiering or of far places; a song; asking to be led to a trader's premises |
| Consequential | reconciling with an enemy; asking for work; inviting someone to travel together; asking for an introduction to someone else; provocation; waking a sleeper; talking out of a crime; and the weightier conversations of a calling: confession, petition, a bribe, asking to be taught |
| Grave | asking a householder, who has found the character waiting in their house at night, to let them stay |

**Example.** A branch of 3 replies, through modi mentis of levels 2, 1 and 2, gives 5 eloquence
dice. The listener is a Distant acquaintance (2 dice), one worn garment flatters their standing
(1 die), and the visage score is 12 (Countenance 2). The pool is 5 + 2 + 1 + 2 = 10 dice. For an
Ordinary request at depth 3 it needs 4 sixes.

### What a conversation teaches

**Rules**

1. A successful conversation teaches three things.
2. First, the general skill of approaching someone and speaking, which is the same whatever follows; this is what the act of approaching teaches.
3. Second, the conversation's own lesson, specific to what was attempted: begging a coin successfully teaches begging.
4. Third, a lesson drawn from the branch actually taken and from who was listening: a request pressed on a person of standing does not teach what the same request pressed on a labourer teaches.
5. Where a branch has no lesson of its own, the conversation's own lesson stands alone.
6. A settled conversation, like a settled act, can move an emotion modus mentis the body holds ([Chapter III §8](03-humors.md#8-emotion-humors)).
7. Alms received, an enmity dropped, an introduction granted, or an exchange that came to nothing are all results an emotion modus mentis can react to.

## 3. Introductions

An introduction turns a Stranger into a Distant acquaintance, and most requests need one first.

**Rules**

1. These need an introduction first: hired work, deepening a relationship, gathering knowledge, inviting someone to travel together, asking to be led to a trader's premises, and asking someone to introduce the character to a third person.
2. Reason for the last: asking someone who does not know the character to vouch for them cannot work in that order.
3. A third person can be asked to introduce the character to someone they stand in a fixed relation to: an apprentice to their master, a worker to whoever runs the holding they work on.
4. That someone must be in the same place and must not yet know the character.
5. Success makes the character already acquainted with them.
6. Every field, grove, cellar and stock place has someone who gives work and someone who can introduce the character to them, so work there is never closed to a stranger who first talks to the hands.
7. Any conversation brought to its end (successful, refused, or walked out of) leaves the other person knowing the character.
8. They become at least a Distant acquaintance, unless the conversation set something worse, as a refusal that costs a step of affinity or walking away does.
9. Trade is the most common such meeting, since it needs no introduction (§4).
10. **Begging** and **provocation** need no introduction, because each is about addressing someone who does not know the character.
11. Reason for rule 10: behind an introduction, begging would leave the destitute nothing to say to anyone, and provocation could not be used on exactly the people worth provoking.
12. Explaining oneself to a witness also needs no introduction, since a witness is as often as not a stranger.

### Conversations of a calling

Some conversations can be held only with people of certain callings. The calling decides whether the
matter can be raised at all; affinity decides only how well it goes.

| Conversation | Only with | Needs introduction | Refusal costs a step of affinity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blessing | the religious | no | no |
| Confession | the religious | yes | yes |
| Petition | a lord, a steward or a captain | no | yes |
| Talk of soldiering | a guard or a captain | yes | no |
| Talk of far places | a merchant or a sailor | yes | no |
| Bribe | a guard, a clerk, a steward or a captain | no | yes |
| Asking to be taught | a scholar, a clerk or a monk | yes | no |
| Joining a song | someone who sings at their work | yes | no |
| Commissioning work | a cobbler, a tailor, a potter, a chandler or a tanner | yes | no |
| Asking a remedy | an apothecary or a barber | no | no |
| Giving alms | a beggar | no | no |
| Town gossip | a laundress, a water-carrier, a porter or a barber | yes | no |

**Rules**

1. The five conversations that need no introduction are by nature addressed to strangers.
2. A blessing is not refused for lack of an introduction; a petition is how an unknown person reaches authority; a bribe goes to exactly the official who does not know the character; a remedy is not withheld from the sick for being unknown; alms go to whoever holds out the bowl.
3. Each of these conversations teaches according to its branch, so what is learned depends on how the matter was put as much as on whether it succeeded.

## 4. Trade

A conversation can open a trader's catalogue, a list of goods to buy or sell.

### Who trades, and where

**Rules**

1. What a person stocks follows from their trade: a catalogue is built from the kinds of goods that trade deals in.
2. Not everything that exists is sold by somebody.
3. People who keep a craft, a shop, a store or a business trade.
4. Soldiers, nobles, clergy, scholars and the street poor do not trade.
5. A large building trades through the one of its people in charge of its stores.
6. A trader's goods are kept on their **premises**, not carried: a workshop's or inn's counter, a farm's barn or shed, a stable's or fold's store, a woodcutter's or fisherman's shed, a large building's entrance hall.
7. A trade can be proposed only while both the trader and the character are on those premises.
8. The same person met elsewhere will talk but will not trade.
9. Trade needs no introduction, since a shop deals with strangers and trade only happens on the premises.
10. Only someone who counts the character an enemy refuses to trade.
11. A trader who knows the character, met away from their premises, can be asked to lead the way there (an Ordinary conversation, §2).
12. If that succeeds, the character is taken to the premises with the trader, at the first period of the day when the trader keeps them: at once if that is now, otherwise later that day, the time passing as in waiting ([Chapter V §8](05-travel.md#8-the-periods-of-the-day)).
13. Every trader is on their premises at least once a day.
14. A shop counter, and the store of a farm or a stock place, is kept by someone able to trade at every period from dawn to evening.
15. A lone worker of the wild keeps their shed for one period only, and must be met there then.

### Prices

**Rules**

1. A trader buys two kinds of goods: what their trade takes in, and what their trade itself sells.
2. What the trade takes in is bought at the trader's full price, as a smith buys ore.
3. What the trade itself sells is bought back at half its reference price (rounded down, at least 1), as a smith buys ironwork.
4. Reason for rule 3: nothing bought from a trader can be sold back to them at a profit.
5. A trader's full price is the item's reference price changed by up to 15 % either way (at least 1 coin either way), then kept between 1 and 100.
6. Each catalogue, buying or selling, is a selection from its kinds of goods and differs from one trader to the next.
7. A kind with 3 items or fewer is offered whole; otherwise 3 to 10 items are offered, most often about 5.
8. Each price is in one coin denomination, and there is **no conversion** between denominations: an item must be paid for in its own kind of coin ([Chapter IV §10](04-items.md#10-coin)).
9. A catalogue line can deliver more than one item, and can be named differently from what appears in the inventory: a bottle of ale arrives as a bottle and an ale.
10. A liquid is sold in a vessel that can hold it, and the vessel's price is part of the line's price.

## 5. Hired work

A conversation can also open an offer of **work**: the character's time traded for coin and
experience. Work is not a task or an errand.

**Rules**

1. Pay is given as the number of days that must be worked to earn 1 coin of the job's denomination, such as a labourer earning 1 silver every 120 days.
2. Reason for rule 1: it keeps poorly paid trades readable without fractions.
3. The coin earned for a stint = days worked ÷ days per coin, rounded down.
4. Every job trains exactly 3 modi mentis, listed most important first.
5. Experience is weighted toward the first, as shown in the table; each share is rounded down.
6. A modus mentis already held gains that experience and rises a level each time it passes the **Experience per Level** threshold, a value derived from the pineal gland score (see [Chapter I §6](01-anatomy.md#6-derived-values)).
7. It stops rising at its maximum level ([Chapter II §3](02-memory.md#3-level-and-maximum-level)).
8. A modus mentis **not** held is learned at level 1 only if the stint's experience for it reaches the threshold in one go; otherwise the stint teaches it nothing.
9. A modus mentis the character has rejected for good is never learned this way ([Chapter II §6](02-memory.md#6-learning-filing-and-losing)).
10. A modus mentis learned this way is filed by the usual procedure of [Chapter II §6](02-memory.md#6-learning-filing-and-losing), and so can push another out.
11. Work moves the calendar on by the days worked; with the end of a journey and the turn from night to dawn, it is one of only three things that do ([Chapter V §7](05-travel.md#7-the-calendar)).

| Modus mentis | Days per point of experience |
|---|---|
| First | 30 |
| Second | 90 |
| Third | 150 |

| Pineal gland score | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Experience per Level | 12 | 12 | 9 | 6 |
| Days to learn the first modus mentis in one stint | 360 | 360 | 270 | 180 |

**Example.** A stint of 300 days gives 10 experience to the first modus mentis, 3 to the second and 2
to the third. With a pineal gland score of 2 (threshold 9), the first is learned at level 1 if it was
not held; the other two are not learned.

## Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Affinity | One person's regard for one party member: 6 ladder levels plus Suspicious |
| Attire | Dice from worn garments that flatter the listener's social standing, up to 6 |
| Countenance | Visage-derived value: the visage score ÷ 5, rounded down, in dice |
| Difficulty | The number of sixes the conversation pool must show |
| Eloquence | Dice from the summed levels of the modi mentis behind the chosen replies |
| Emotion modus mentis | A modus mentis that reacts to the result of an act or conversation |
| Enmity | Being counted an enemy; separate from affinity |
| Experience per Level | Pineal-gland-derived value: the experience needed for each level; lower is better |
| Introduction | The conversation that turns a Stranger into a Distant acquaintance |
| Premises | The place where a trader keeps their goods and will trade |
| Reference price | An item's base price, from which traders' prices are set |
| Resolution | The single dice check at the end of a conversation branch |
| Score | An organ's or body region's number, the sum of its parts ([Chapter I](01-anatomy.md)) |
| Speaking modus mentis | A modus mentis that supplies replies in conversation |
| Speech Fluency | Tongue-derived value: the number of replies offered, 0 to 5 |
| Suspicious | The affinity state after reconciliation: 0 dice, off the ladder |
| Viscera | The trunk organ whose score caps how many humors can be spent on one roll |
