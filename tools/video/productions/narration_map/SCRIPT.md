# Narration on the map: narration

Generated by build_storyboard.py. Explanation is plain; *the game's own text, read in the second voice, is in italics*; [Scene] marks a manim scene.

Proscribed Palimpsest has a new narration screen. The page of text with clickable words is gone. Instead, you see the place itself: a map you read, click, and fill as you play.
This video shows why it changed, and how it plays. The rules are now easier to see, so it also looks at what each rule is for: what the minds do, how a will finds its way, and what a crime costs.

The old screen had its charm. When you arrived somewhere, a mind wrote a paragraph about it. Some words lit up, and you clicked a word to look closer. It read a bit like a book. But a book is not a good place to stand in. You could not see where things were. You could not tell where a word would lead. And the text scrolled away, often just when you needed it again.

To act on the farmer, you clicked the word farmer. Then you chose what to do. Then you chose which mind would do it. Three menus, but only one was a real choice: which mind. The new screen keeps only that one.

**[Principles]**
Now the place is the page. Each area is a card, and each way is a line between two cards. Under each card is a row of symbols: the things you can look at. Think of it as a mind map of the place. The visit grows on it. You click one symbol, and the mind you choose looks at that thing. The symbol moves under its card and becomes a root, with the text the mind wrote. Think about it, and a branch grows. It shows what the mind wants, and the act it proposes, with the sixes it needs. Do the act, and the branch ends with the result, and how it made you feel. The old page scrolled away as you read. Here nothing does. Every text stays where it grew. As the visit goes on, trees fill the map, and you can read the whole visit at a glance. Walking back where you have been is free. A way you have never taken is an act, like any other.
That is the whole idea. The screen should look like what happens underneath: minds working on a place, and leaving marks where they worked. At the end of a visit, the map should tell you what happened there.

Here is the same farm on the new screen. Each card is an area. The lit card is where we stand. The lines are the ways between areas. At the top, the header shows our name and our six noetic points. And one symbol up there is the way out.

**[Legend kinds]**
Each card is an area. Under its name is a row of symbols, one for each thing in it. There are four kinds. An arch is a way out: a neighbouring area, a door, a stair, a path or a climb. A face is a person, or any creature that is not your enemy. A skull is an enemy. A diamond is any other thing: a cart, a bush, a chest, a body. A hollow diamond is something seen inside a container.
Only four kinds, on purpose. A symbol tells you what a click will look at, not what you will find. Finding out is still the mind's job.

**[Legend states]**
Each thing in a place is in one of three states, for each member of the party. Unknown: you only know its kind and where it is. You click the kind, and the mind you choose picks which one to look at. Known: you remember it from an earlier visit, or saw it inside a container. It has a name, and a click looks at that one. Observed: you looked at it on this visit. You can only think about what you observed, and it becomes the root of a tree.
That last rule may be the most important. You cannot plan around something nobody has looked at. So every tree starts with a mind paying attention, and paying for it.

**[Legend tree]**
A tree holds everything the visit wrote about one thing. First the root, and under it the text the observing mind wrote. Then a question mark, to think about it, as long as there is something to do with it. Then the thought, and the act it proposed, with the number of sixes it needs. You can click it whenever you like. Then the result: a tick or a cross, and how it made you feel. A refusal, from any mind, for any reason, is a crossed circle. A quotation mark is something told to a companion. Hover over any node to read its text again.
Nothing in a tree is decoration. Every node is something that happened. And it stays there as long as you are in the place.

Let's play. In the Courtyard there is one person we don't know yet. We click it. The popup asks only one thing: which mind will look. We pick Lullaby.
> *I pass by fields where the corn whispers secrets to the wind, my voice a gentle hum that soothes the beating hearts of these gentle creatures. Nothing here draws my attention, only the soft, steady beat of the world around me.*
Lullaby says no. Minds have tastes, and a mind can say this kind of thing does not interest it. That still costs the point, and the refusal stays on the map as a crossed circle. But each thing can only be refused once. A mind's taste can shape a visit, but it should not block you.

We click again. Lullaby is now greyed out in the popup. Whichever mind we ask now has to look. We pick Social Interaction.
> *I find my gaze drawn to a butterfly, a broad-winged chalk-blue one, lifting away effortlessly on the warm breeze.*
It's a butterfly. The symbol moves under the card and becomes the root of a tree. The text is under it, with a question mark: something to think about.

**[Chain steps]**
The rules of an act did not change. An act still takes three minds. One mind looks. You must be in the area, and it costs one noetic point. One mind thinks, and decides what it wants. It can think from anywhere in the place, and it costs one point. One mind acts. The body walks there first, and the act itself costs nothing more.
Your noetic points come from your brain. A success refills all of them, right away. A failure refills nothing. With no points left, you can still walk where you know the way, do the acts already waiting, and leave.
These costs set the pace. Looking and thinking take attention, so they cost something. A success refills everything, to reward doing over thinking. A visit that goes well keeps going. One that goes badly slowly runs out of breath.

**[Functions]**
So which mind does what? Each mind has one to three functions, out of eight. It can observe, think, act, speak, fight, feel, want, or know a trade. No mind both thinks and acts. No mind both acts and feels. Wanting something, doing it, and being moved by it are three minds, not one.
The will is the one to understand. A will does not weigh options. It wants a few kinds of act, and it only looks and thinks in that direction.
This split tries to copy how a mind might work. The part of you that wants a drink may not be the part that pours it. And neither is the part that feels bad about it later. Here, each of those is a mind, and you choose which one to listen to.

**[Properties]**
Each mind also has a few properties, and each one changes how it behaves on the map. Here are three real minds. Beast Sense looks and thinks. Petty Thief is a will to steal, and it acts. Diligence is a will to work, and it speaks and acts.
Its sources, a body region or two organs, set how high its level can go. A wound lowers it. Its memory type says where it can be stored. Its moral level, low, medium or high, only matters when it thinks or acts. Its doubt says how many second thoughts it gets. And a discreet mind can act with someone in the room, and is not heard through a wall.
None of these is really a number to raise. They are there so that two minds can act differently with the same thing, and so you can see why.

Now a will. Ratcatcher wants one thing: to catch vermin. We ask it to think about the butterfly.
> *I spy a broad-winged chalk-blue butterfly, its flight almost ethereal. I am drawn to capture its grace, but first, I must learn the small tricks of trapping and stalking, as this butterfly might be found in hidden nooks and crannies around our farm.*
> *I will patiently catch the butterfly with my hands, sharp eyes read each leaf for signs of its flight.*
To Ratcatcher, a butterfly is small game. That is the charm of a will: it sees the whole world through one want. Here, its course is a single act: catch it. That act now waits under the tree. Hunt was chosen to do it.

**[Chain dice]**
You roll one die for each level of each mind in the chain: the one that looked, the one that thought, and the one that acts. A tool adds its own level. The difficulty is how many sixes you need. An eager mind needs one six less. A reluctant mind needs one more. If you need more sixes than you have dice, the act cannot succeed. And an act of difficulty zero needs no roll.
That may be the point of the chain. A good roll should come from picking the right mind for each step, not from one big number.

Hover over that act, and the map shows its dice and the sixes it needs. It needs four sixes, but the chain only gives three dice. It cannot succeed, so we leave it. Let's try a way instead. Each way has its own symbol, on its line. You choose exactly where to explore. No mind picks the way for you. We ask Heraldry to look at the way to the Chicken Coop.
> *I behold no need for a chicken coop here, and my eye wanders to other matters.*
Another refusal. Each way can be refused once.

So the next mind has to look. Childhood Memory.
> *I recall my childhood barn, where the smell of damp hay and hay bales was just as strong. Now, here is this cramped coop filled with chickens, their feathers wet from dewy mornings.*

Ratcatcher again, about the way. Will it go in?
> *I notice the coop is crowded with damp feathers and droppings, a clear sign of vermin. To catch them, I must step into the coop and then the courtyard, using trapping and stalking as a small game hunter.*
> *I will quietly enter the chicken coop*
Yes. The coop is on its course, and a will is eager to follow its own course. A plain way normally needs one six. Eager, it needs none. Following a will should be easy: that is what wills are for. And look at the header: that thought used our last point.

With no points left, we can still do an act that is already waiting. An empty pool should not be a dead end. We click the act. It needs no sixes, so there is no roll.
> *I found my path, stepped into the coop, and learned the way between the rows of chickens.*
Success. The result box says so, and lists what happened, one line each. Look at the header: a success refills all our points. Six again. And taking a way for the first time taught us Wayfaring. From now on, this way is known, and walking it is free.

In the coop, we ask Ratcatcher to look at the people. A will only looks at the first step of its course, and nothing here is one.
> *I see no sign of life here, just the usual pests and vermin. To catch what I want, I must remain hidden and observe, like an unseen hunter in the upturned cart.*
Then we ask Hunt. A will's refusal does not count as the one refusal a thing allows. So Hunt can still refuse, and it does.
> *I do not see anything that draws my attention here, but I sharpen my eye to find what might be taken.*

Third try: now the mind has to look. Sloth is a low-moral mind that observes and thinks.
> *I catch a glimpse of a small russet hen, clucking and scratching in the dirt, her presence heavy with the weight of the day.*

This may be the most telling moment of the run. Watch what low morals do. We ask Sloth to think about the hen.
> *I notice a small russet hen, clucking and scratching in the dirt. Tomorrow, perhaps, when the sun is lower, I will rely on the steady finishing of what was begun.*
The game log explains it. Sloth is unscrupulous, so of the seven possible goals, it is only offered the two illegal ones. Hitting a hen that is not wild is a crime: the bird belongs to someone. Sloth chose to attack it. Then comes the choice of who does it. Diligence is a principled mind, so it refuses the crime before it is even offered.
> *I don't want to handle the chicken today. It goes against the careful, steady way I do all my work here.*

**[Morality]**
Morality is checked twice, because choosing a goal and choosing who does it are two decisions. A high-moral thinking mind is never offered a crime. A high-moral acting mind refuses a crime before it is even offered. A low-moral thinking mind, when a crime is possible, is offered only crimes, and must pick one. And a low-moral acting mind cannot refuse a crime at all. So an honest hand can still be chosen to carry out what a low mind decided.
The idea is that morality belongs to the minds, not to the character. Your character has no alignment. It has an honest mind and a greedy one. Which one you listen to, at which step, is your choice.

**[Rules]**
Two kinds of rule can stop an act. Rules of action judge an act you already chose. They refuse it, in the acting mind's own voice, and that costs a point. That man is watching. That door needs a key. My arm is ruined. Rules of choice decide what is offered at all. They hide options silently, and cost nothing. A principled mind is never offered a crime. A speaking mind turns to people. The idea simply never occurs to it.
The difference matters. A refusal when you act is the world pushing back, so it seems fair that it costs. Something that never crosses a mind is just who that mind is. It would be strange to pay for that.

Two more looks. Gourmandise refuses the things in the coop. Heraldry, asked next, has to look, and finds the nest box.
> *I don't see anything here that promises a good meal.*
> *I notice a nest box, a cozy row of straw-lined boxes where the hens lay their eggs.*
Our points are gone again. This member can do nothing more until an act succeeds.

**[Party]**
The party changed the most. Each member is now a separate body in the place. It stays where you last took it. It spends its own points, uses its own minds, and keeps its own trees. Click a token to switch member, for free. A member with a speaking mind can tell a nearby companion about something it observed. That costs the speaker a point, and puts that thing on the listener's map, already observed. A beast gets no conversation, no lock, no tool and no letters. And a fight only involves the members standing where it starts.
This is probably the biggest change. Companions used to be like extra minds for the hero. Now they are people standing somewhere else. Splitting the party becomes a real decision.

But a member is only one body. We click the other token: Raggedbrow, still in the Courtyard. He has his own three points, his own minds, and his own map, which is empty. He knows the butterfly's name, because the party has seen it. But he has not observed it himself. Caution refuses. Curiosity looks.
> *I am not drawn to the butterfly, and I pause to note the slight wrongness that persists.*
> *I find a broad-winged chalk-blue butterfly lifting away on nothing. Its wings are broad, and it's a chalk-blue, drawing my gaze.*

Curiosity wants to examine it closely.
> *I spy a broad-winged chalk-blue fluttering into the air, its wings barely touching the breeze. What secrets does it hide? I must catch it, press it flat against the soil, and uncover its mysteries before time runs out.*
We pick Digging to do it, and it refuses at once. Digging does not observe, so it will not stand and look. That is a rule of choice, and the game tells you why.
> *I don't want to stand and observe the butterfly closely here. I want to delve into its patterns, act with purpose, not just stand and watch.*

Everybody is out of points, so we leave. Leave walks every member out, from where they stand, by the ways they know. That is also why Go Back is gone: walking back where you have been should cost nothing.

**[Will course]**
Take Petty Thief. It wants one thing: to steal. The game works out its course. That is the shortest chain of acts from here to a theft: open the shop door, walk to the shelf, steal. Asked to look, it only looks at the first step of that course. Asked about anything else, it says no, and names the first step. That refusal is part of its nature, and costs nothing. And when it does what it wants, it is eager: it needs one six less, and it never refuses.
A will is single-minded because a want probably is. It does not compare options. It sees what leads to what it wants, and ignores the rest. That makes a will a poor observer, but maybe a very good guide.

**[Will candidates]**
When it thinks, a will is less strict. Any act that starts a course of three acts or fewer is a candidate. It proposes the shortest one. A second thought takes the next. Longer courses are ignored, unless the place has nothing shorter.

A cobbler's shop, in the city. Five things, two people. We ask Petty Thief to look at a thing. As a will, it goes straight to the first step of its course.
> *I spy a rack of lasts, dark-handled wooden feet for shoes, each chalked with names. That's my chance to lift one, easy as pie.*

Diligence wants work: a job, a bench, supplies to stock. We ask it to think about the same rack.
> *I notice the labelled wooden feet, each chalked with a customer's name, and I find myself drawn to find work, to seek a bench where I can take in honest tasks waiting to be done. Yet, it seems no such place exists here, and I must leave this place to find where my efforts are truly valued in this old world.*
Nothing here leads to what it wants, so it refuses, and says it would have to leave. Diligence is also principled, so it would never be offered the theft anyway. This kind of refusal is not really a dead end. It tells you what this mind is looking for.

**[Will leave]**
And when the place offers no course at all, the will refuses everything. It says it would have to leave to find what it wants. That is not a dead end. It is a hint. From the world map, you can send a will to search. It tries the nearest places first. The first place where its course exists becomes your destination. The visit is then made for that want, however long the course.
This may be the new way to find things. Not by wandering until something happens, but by listening to what your minds want, and letting them lead.

Petty Thief thinks about the rack.
> *I notice the labelled wooden feet on pegs, each chalked with a name. Last, I'll pick a shoe, relying on the picking of pockets and small thefts.*
> *I will discreetly steal a shoe last, as seen on the crowded cobblestones.*
A theft, needing two sixes. A will cannot refuse what it wants, and a low-moral mind cannot refuse a crime. The cobbler is in the room. But Petty Thief is discreet, and a discreet mind may try.

**[Proximity raw]**
Being seen raises two separate questions. First: can I act at all? With someone in the room, an ordinary mind is refused. That is a rule of action, so it costs a point. A discreet mind can try. That is all discretion gives you up close. And a discreet mind cannot back out of an act that only its discretion allows.

We do it. Three dice, one six. It fails.
> *I know not, thought I saw a shoe last time, but no luck. Lost the flow, just like always.*
In the same room, a failure is seen. We are caught red-handed. This is where a crime should hurt.

**[Proximity effective]**
Second: what does failing cost? There are three levels. In the same room, a witness catches you, and an enemy attacks. Behind a wall, in the same building, they come to look. They walk into your room, and now someone is there. Further away, nothing happens. Discretion keeps you quiet through a wall, but never in the same room.
Discretion is the only property that bends these rules, so it is kept narrow. It lets you try. It never saves you from what failing costs, face to face.

Being caught starts a conversation. Losing it starts a fight.
> *There, stop. I have seen you take what's not yours.*
We answer. Each reply on offer is written by one of our minds, with its name in front. Diligence denies it.
> *Nay, it was not me, nor doth my guilt besmirch your tale.*
> *It's someone else's fault. That's always the case. I'm listening, and it better be worth it.*
Then Social Interaction answers.
> *Though I confess, my hands were not stained with this theft, yet I shall share with you what I can to clear my name.*
> *Whose fault is this? Name the culprit or I'll have none of this nonsense.*
> *I was informed I had permission. Should they have deceived me, the blame lies with those who gave the order.*
That reply is a check. We roll five dice, from the minds that spoke, our looks, and our coat. We need four sixes, and get one. We spend a humor to turn a die into a six. That makes two. Not enough.
> *Answer my insult with silence, for I expect no less.*
We lose the conversation, and the cobbler attacks. On the map, the exit now says Runaway.

**[Wages]**
A failure costs three things. Your points are not refilled. There can be one social consequence: you are caught, attacked, or someone comes to look. And there is one penalty, based on what the act risked. A wound, for something that can hurt you. A lost way, for finding a way: the visit ends and you are outside. A lost hour, for hard and slow work. Or a lost thread, one more noetic point, for the senses, speech and waiting.
Each failure tries to cost something that fits it. A failed climb hurts. A failed search loses the way. A failed conversation loses the thread. Ideally, you can guess the price before you roll.

Same shop, another try. This time Sloth thought of the theft, and Petty Thief was chosen to do it. The dice are the same, five, six, four. But this time we use a humor to turn one into a six. Success: a shoe last goes in the pack, and our points refill.
> *I got a shoe, didn't I? Walked right out of there. Thanks to this little score, I'm feeling pretty slick.*

Under the rack there is a quotation mark: tell someone about it. Social Interaction tells Raggedbrow, who is standing nearby.
> *Raggedbrow, come over now! I spied those dark-handled wooden feet, all sizes, neatly hung on pegs. Each one was marked with a customer's name in chalk. What a fascinating detail!*
That cost us a point. Switch to Raggedbrow, and the rack is on his map, already observed, with our words as his description.

Back to us. We look at the cobbler, and ask Sloth about him.
> *I find myself drawn to the cobbler of the city, a man with an old army boot over his knee, hammering in hobnails without looking up. It's thick and entirely untroubled by the process.*
> *I notice the cobbler in the city, a man skilled at his craft. Who insists on attacking him? No rush, who needs this trouble?*
> *I will meet him head-on, heart and hunger set aflame in this steel-and-brick city.*
Only crimes again. This time it is an assault, and Vigor is chosen to do it. Vigor is not discreet.

We do it.
> *I cannot step into that room, for there I meet him. The cobbler is here, and I would be caught red-handed, so I leave it undone. Hearty and eager, I see the task ahead with appetite, but this one must wait, for the cobbler stands between.*
A rule of action stops it. The cobbler is in the room, and an ordinary mind is refused. We chose this act, so the refusal costs a point, and the act keeps waiting.

Reconsider asks a thinking mind to look at the act again. It keeps the goal, but gives the act to a different mind, one that no rule refuses here: a discreet one.
> *I think again about how to bypass him, but another path seems more suitable. I will proceed with stealth, picking pockets and small thefts, as it requires less effort and fuss.*
> *I will softly strike him there, in the crowded square.*
The old act stays, crossed out, and the new one waits: Petty Thief, needing one six. Hitting him would start a fight, so we leave it there.

**[Confronted closed]**
An enemy in the room confronts you. So does a witness with a reason to stop you. The map then closes around them. You can answer them. Explaining yourself makes every check of that conversation one six easier. Or you can do an act already waiting, if a discreet mind will. A thinking mind is only offered those answers, unless a discreet mind could act. You cannot look at anything else, think about anything else, walk away, or talk to a companion. And someone asleep confronts nobody.
Someone who just caught you stealing will not give you time to look around the shop. When you are cornered, the screen tries to corner you too.

To show the rest, a debug option makes everyone treat us as an enemy. We walk into the shop. The cobbler is a skull, already on our map. While he confronts us, he is the only thing we can look at or think about.

The exit says Runaway. We roll the feet's two dice and need one six. A five and a six. We are out. Raggedbrow is still in the shop, but the cobbler's problem is with us, not him. Nothing stops him, so his exit says Leave.

**[Confronted exit]**
The way out is one symbol. When nobody stops you, it says Leave. Every member walks out by known ways, for free. If a walk meets someone who would confront you, it stops there. When you are confronted, it says Runaway. You roll your feet's dice and need a single six. The humors can help. The roll is made again in every area on the way, and failing it starts the fight, or the conversation.

That is narration on the map. The rules underneath did not change. The minds, the dice, the wills and the crimes are the same. What changed is that you can see them, and see what your minds did in a place. Hopefully, that makes their choices, and yours, a little easier to follow. Thank you for playing.
