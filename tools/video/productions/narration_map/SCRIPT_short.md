# Narration on the map: narration

Generated by build_storyboard.py. Explanation is plain; *the game's own text, read in the second voice, is in italics*; [Scene] marks a manim scene.

Proscribed Palimpsest has a new narration screen. The page of text with its clickable words is gone. In its place, the place itself: a map you read, click and fill as you play.
What follows: why it changed, and what the rules underneath it are trying to do: what a mind is, how a will finds its way, and what a crime should cost.

The old screen had its charm. Arriving at a place, a mind wrote a paragraph about it, some words in it lit up, and you clicked a word to look closer. It read a little like a book. But a book is perhaps a poor place to stand in. You could not quite see where things were, nor tell what a word would lead to, and what you read scrolled away, often just when it was needed again.

**[Principles]**
Now the place is the page. Every area is a card, every way a line between them, and under each card stands a row of symbols: what there is to look at. Think of it as a mind map of the place, and the visit grows on it. You click one symbol, one thing, and the mind you choose looks at it. The symbol drops under its card and becomes a root, with the text the mind wrote. Think about it, and a branch grows: what the mind wants, and the act it proposes, waiting, with the sixes it needs. Carry it out, and the branch ends in what it came to, and what it stirred. The old page scrolled away as you read it. Here nothing does: every text stays where it grew, and as the visit goes on, trees fill the map, the whole visit readable at a glance. And where you have been, you walk again for free. A way not yet taken is an act like any other.
That is more or less the whole idea: a screen that looks like what happens underneath, minds at work on a place, leaving their marks where they worked. By the end of a visit, the map should read almost like an account of what happened there.

**[Functions]**
So which mind is asked what? Every mind bears one to three functions, out of eight. It observes, it thinks, it acts, it speaks, it fights, it feels, it wants, or it knows a trade. Thinking and action are never in the same mind, and neither are action and emotion: to want a thing, to do it and to be moved by it are three minds, not one.
The will is the one to understand. A will does not weigh options. It wants a few kinds of act, and it observes and thinks only toward them.
This split tries to mimic how a mind might work. The part of you that wants a drink is maybe not the part that pours it, nor the part that feels bad about it later. Here, each of those is a mind, and the one you listen to is up to you.

Here is the farm, played. Every mind is chosen by hand, and some minds are wills. Ratcatcher wants one thing: to catch vermin. We ask it to think about a butterfly.
> *I spy a broad-winged chalk-blue butterfly, its flight almost ethereal. I am drawn to capture its grace, but first, I must learn the small tricks of trapping and stalking, as this butterfly might be found in hidden nooks and crannies around our farm.*
> *I will patiently catch the butterfly with my hands, sharp eyes read each leaf for signs of its flight.*
To Ratcatcher, a butterfly is small game. That is rather the charm of wills: they see the whole world through one want. Its course here is a single act, catching it, which now waits under the tree. Hunt was chosen to carry it out.

**[Will leave]**
And when no course exists in the place at all, the will declines everything, and says it would have to leave to find what it wants. That is not a dead end: it is a hint. From the world map, a will can be sent to search: the places in range are tried nearest first, and the first where its course exists becomes the journey's end. The visit is then made for that want, however long the course.
This may be the way to find things now. Less by wandering until something happens, more by listening to what your minds want, and letting them lead.

Now a mind with low morals: Sloth. We ask it to think about a hen.
> *I notice a small russet hen, clucking and scratching in the dirt. Tomorrow, perhaps, when the sun is lower, I will rely on the steady finishing of what was begun.*
Behind that thought, the game log says: Sloth is unscrupulous, offering only the two illegal goals of seven. Striking a hen that is not wild is a crime: the bird is somebody's. Sloth chose to attack it. Then the means: Diligence, a principled mind, refuses a crime before it is even offered.
> *I don't want to handle the chicken today. It goes against the careful, steady way I do all my work here.*

A cobbler's shop, in the city. Petty Thief, a will to steal, thinks about a rack of shoes.
> *I notice the labelled wooden feet on pegs, each chalked with a name. Last, I'll pick a shoe, relying on the picking of pockets and small thefts.*
> *I will discreetly steal a shoe last, as seen on the crowded cobblestones.*
A theft, difficulty two. A will cannot refuse an act it wants, and an unscrupulous one cannot refuse a crime. The cobbler is in the room, but Petty Thief is discreet, and a discreet mind may try.

Execute. Three dice, one six. It fails.
> *I know not, thought I saw a shoe last time, but no luck. Lost the flow, just like always.*
And in the same room, a failure is seen. Caught red-handed. This is where a crime is meant to bite.

A confrontation is a conversation, and losing it is a fight.
> *There, stop. I have seen you take what's not yours.*
We answer. Every reply on offer is written by one of our minds, its name before it. Diligence denies it.
> *Nay, it was not me, nor doth my guilt besmirch your tale.*
> *It's someone else's fault. That's always the case. I'm listening, and it better be worth it.*
Social Interaction answers next.
> *Though I confess, my hands were not stained with this theft, yet I shall share with you what I can to clear my name.*
> *Whose fault is this? Name the culprit or I'll have none of this nonsense.*
> *I was informed I had permission. Should they have deceived me, the blame lies with those who gave the order.*
That reply is a check. Five dice: the levels of the minds that spoke, our looks, our coat. Four sixes needed, and one comes up. We spend a humor, and a die turns to six: two. Not enough.
> *Answer my insult with silence, for I expect no less.*
The conversation is lost, and the cobbler fights. On the map, the exit now reads Runaway.

That is narration on the map. The rules underneath did not change: the minds, the dice, the wills and the crimes are as they were. What changed is that you can see them, and see what your minds made of a place. Hopefully it makes their choices, and yours, a little easier to read. Thank you for playing.
