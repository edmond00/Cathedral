This is Proscribed Palimpsest, a role-playing game where a body, its ways of thinking, and a language model tell the story together. In this video we play the first hour of a run, from the main menu to a pair of new boots. Each system is explained as it comes up: first what it is for, then how it works, then what it did in this run. And along the way, I'll tell you what I would do as a player.

A run starts by choosing a world. Each moon in this sky is one: three hundred and eighty-three of them, each with a name. The sky is fixed, the same on every machine, so a moon always stands for the same world. Two players who pick the same moon walk the same land and meet the same people, so they can compare notes.

Choosing a moon generates its world, and its history comes first. Realms rise and fall; castles, temples and cities are founded; then farmland, villages and pastures spread out from them. The card says what kind of world it is. We take Fenumoth, a wooded world. For a first run, any moon will do: every one of them is built to be livable.

Now the protagonist. There are no strength or charisma scores in this game. A character is a body: five regions, which are the head, the face, the trunk, the arms and the legs; twenty-two organs; twenty-eight organ parts. Every part starts at one point, and you get twenty-five more to spend. That buys less than half of what the body could reach, so creating a character is really choosing what to give up.

**[Organs]**
Every number the game uses comes from one organ or one region. This map groups them by the system they feed: thinking and memory, the senses, speech, the humors, life, and the body in a fight. In the game, hovering an organ shows the same information on the right of the screen.
We'll go through them in that order. Watch where the points go: this character is built to think, to talk, and to bend the dice.

First, thinking and memory: the head. As a whole, the head sets your noetic points, how many times you can look and think in one scene: its score halved, plus one.
What you learn in this game are called modi mentis: ways of thinking. A new one always arrives in working memory, a short queue, also sized by the whole head. When that queue is full, the oldest way of thinking falls out, and it is lost for good.
The anamnesis keeps a second queue, for what you set aside, and it decides how many places you remember: four per point. We raise it by one.

To keep a way of thinking for good, you file it into a long-term memory of its kind. The cerebrum holds knowledge, like bargaining or archaeology. We give it two points.

The cerebellum holds practised skills, like swordsmanship or knotwork, and it is what lets you learn a new move in the middle of a fight. Two points as well.

The hippocampus holds the ways of thinking that use the senses.

And the pineal gland sets how much experience a level costs, from twelve points down to six. With two points or more, it also lets a mind doubt its own plan and think again.
My advice: don't neglect memory. A character who learns a lot but has nowhere to file it keeps forgetting.

The senses. The eyes set the reach of a ranged blow, in cells.

The ears add their score to your initiative, which decides who acts first in a fight. We raise both.

The nose is underrated. Each point lowers the chance of meeting danger on the road by ten per cent, and it tells you which humors are in a food before you eat it.

Speech. The tongue sets how many replies you are offered in a conversation, up to five. At nought, you cannot hold a conversation at all. Two points here: conversation is how you get work, trade and friends.

The teeth only decide whether you can eat hard food. We leave them.

The humors are this game's most unusual system. Four organs make them: the paunch, the hepar, the spleen and the pulmones. Each keeps a queue of forty-nine humors. A sound organ makes mostly blood; a failing one, mostly black bile.
Humors are your fuel: every step of a journey burns some of their warmth. And they are your luck: a humor can change a die after it has been rolled.

The viscera decide how many humors you may spend on a single roll, and how fast wounds close. Four points here, for five in all. I would always do this: it is the difference between a failed roll and a saved one.

Life. The heart sets how long you live, how many companions you can lead, one per point, and how many youthful humors you start with, which are warm and lucky. Four points: a long life, and room for a party later.

The genitories add dice to every blow you strike. One point.

The body in a fight. The backbone sets how much you can carry, and your movement points in a fight: twice its score. Three more points.

The hands decide which tools you can use. A practised hand does not get more out of a tool; it can make more things serve as tools. Two points for the right hand.

In a fight, each hand and each foot strikes on its own, and the arms are the organs of brute force.

The legs set how far one movement point takes you. With the feet, the lower body as a whole sets how far one journey can reach, and your natural defence.

The feet keep you standing on bad ground, and roll the dice to run away. One last number: health. It comes from the trunk as a whole, a third of its score plus one, and each wound costs a point of it until it heals. Twenty-five points spent. That is the body.

The body has no past yet. The game gives it one through five childhood memories, and they are played exactly the way the whole game is played. So the first memory is our first lesson. Let's take it slowly.
> *I almost have it, a half-remembered weeping, sobs like faded echoes in the dark, a woman's voice somewhere, half-formed in the hush of old iron and myth.*

That text was not written in advance. It was written a moment ago, by a language model running on this computer. It runs entirely on your machine: no account, no server, nothing sent anywhere, and it works on the processor alone.
But the model does not run the game. The rules, the dice, wounds, coin and time are ordinary code. The model is asked narrow questions inside that code: what does this mind notice, what does it want, how does it tell what happened.
Each question is put to a modus mentis, a way of thinking, and the model plays that way of thinking as a character, in its own voice. That is why every passage is headed by a name. This one is Childhood Reminiscence.

**[DiceHumors]**
Every roll works the same way. You roll one die for each level of every mind in the chain: the one that observed, the one that thought, and the one that acts. The difficulty is the number of sixes you need. So raising any of the three minds means more dice, and better odds.
When a roll falls short, the humors can save it. Only the humor at the back of each queue can be spent, one per die, and only as many in one roll as the viscera allow. Blood adds one to a die. Yellow bile takes one away. Phlegm does nothing, but spending it clears the way to the humor behind it. Black bile does nothing, and never leaves. A queue full of black bile is spent, and when all four are, the body dies. So humors are your luck, and also your life.
