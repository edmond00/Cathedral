Your character in this game is not a sheet of numbers. It is a body, built organ by organ, and a crowd of minds living in it: some notice, some think, some act, and some only feel. Each one speaks in its own voice, and nothing they say is written in advance.

This is Proscribed Palimpsest. In this video we play the start of a run, from the main menu to a pair of new boots, and we'll see how all of it works. And along the way, I'll tell you what I would do as a player.

A run starts by choosing a world. Each moon in this sky is one: three hundred and eighty-three of them, each with a name. The sky is fixed, the same on every machine, so a moon always stands for the same world. Two players who pick the same moon walk the same land and meet the same people, so they can compare notes.

Choosing a moon generates its world, and its history comes first. Realms rise and fall; castles, temples and cities are founded; then farmland, villages and pastures spread out from them. The card says what kind of world it is. We take Fenumoth, a wooded world. For a first run, any moon will do: every one of them is built to be livable.

Now the protagonist. There are no strength or charisma scores in this game. A character is a body: five regions, which are the head, the face, the trunk, the arms and the legs; twenty-two organs; twenty-eight organ parts. Every part starts at one point, and you get twenty-five more to spend. That buys less than half of what the body could reach, so creating a character is really choosing what to give up.

Before we spend them, here is how a character fits together, in four ideas. First, the body: twenty-two organs, grouped in five regions, the head, the face, the trunk, the arms and the legs. A region scores the sum of its organs, but the organs are not just parts of a total: most rules read one organ on its own, the ears for initiative, the backbone for movement, the heart for your lifespan, and only some read a whole region. Here are the regions for the character we are about to build.

**[System]**
Second, the measures. Each one is computed from a single organ or region, by a rule of its own. The backbone, doubled, gives the movement points you spend in a fight: four makes eight. The head, halved and plus one, gives your noetic points, the thoughts you can have in one scene: ten makes six.

**[System]**
Third, the ways of thinking, called modi mentis. Think of them as the skills of a classic role-playing game: Swordsmanship, Bargaining, Archaeology. Like skills, they have levels, they gain experience when they are used, and their level is what you roll. Unlike skills, you do not buy them on a sheet: you learn them by living, from a memory, a job, a fight, and you can forget them. And each one is also a voice, a mind with its own manner, that notices, thinks or acts for you, and writes in its own style.
The body sets how far each can grow. A way of thinking draws on two organs, or on one region, and those set its ceiling: one, plus up to three for each organ, or up to six for a region, the stronger they are. Swordsmanship draws on the arms and the hands. The arms score two out of four: plus two. The hands, four out of six: plus two. So its ceiling is five. Hover any organ during creation, and the screen lists the ceilings it raises.

Fourth, the dice. Almost everything you attempt, a blow, a question, a climb, is settled by a roll. You roll one die for each level of the ways of thinking involved, and only sixes count: the task says how many it needs. Swordsmanship is level two today, so it brings two dice. Raise it, and it brings more.

**[Memory]**
Memory deserves a closer look, because this game makes you forget. Everything you learn arrives in working memory, at the front. Working memory is a queue: when it is full and something new arrives, the oldest way of thinking falls out of the back, and it is lost for good.

**[Memory]**
So you file what you want to keep. Consolidating moves a way of thinking into the long-term memory of its kind, practised skills, knowledge, or the senses, and there it is safe. Or you reject one on the spot, to make room before the queue decides for you.

**[Memory]**
Long-term memories fill up too. To make room, you archive a way of thinking into the residual memory, a last queue the anamnesis keeps, and each memory always keeps at least one. The humors are this game's most unusual system. Four organs of the trunk make them: the pulmones, the paunch, the spleen and the hepar. Each keeps a queue of forty-nine humors. A sound organ makes mostly blood; a failing one, mostly black bile.

**[DiceHumors]**
Every roll works the same way. You roll one die for each level of every mind in the chain: the one that observed, the one that thought, and the one that acts. The difficulty is the number of sixes you need. So raising any of the three minds means more dice, and better odds.
